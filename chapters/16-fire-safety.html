Chapter 16

Fire safety systems

Fire systems are the one part of a building where the BMS is a guest. They are certified, self-contained and legally inspected. This chapter explains how detection loops, control matrices, smoke control and voice evacuation work, and where the line sits between what a BMS may see and what it must never touch.

16.1 Fire alarm systems (SSP): detectors, loops, panels

A fire detection and fire alarm system finds a fire early, tells people and the fire brigade, and triggers fire protection equipment. In Poland it is called SSP (system sygnalizacji pożarowej). Elsewhere you will see FDAS, FAS or simply "fire alarm". In Europe the components are covered by the EN 54 family of standards. Each part covers one device type.

Design and installation rules are national or come from codes of practice: for example BS 5839-1 in the UK, VdS 2095 and DIN VDE 0833-2 in Germany, NFPA 72 in the USA, and CEN/TS 54-14 as a European guide. In Poland products need certification (CNBOP-PIB) and designs follow national guidelines and the fire safety regulation.

Conventional vs addressable

A conventional system wires detectors in parallel on a zone circuit. A detector in alarm draws extra current, and the panel only knows "zone 3 is in alarm". An addressable system puts every device on a digital loop. Each device has an address. The panel polls each one, reads analog values (smoke obscuration, temperature, dirt compensation) and knows exactly which detector fired: "Loop 1, address 047, 2nd floor, room 214".

A typical loop carries up to about 125 to 250 devices depending on the vendor protocol, over a 2-wire cable of up to roughly 2 to 3.5 km. The cable leaves the panel and returns to it. That is the trick: the panel can feed the loop from both ends. A single break loses nothing.

A short circuit is worse. It collapses the voltage on the whole loop. That is why loops carry short-circuit isolators (EN 54-17), either as separate modules or built into detector bases. When a short appears, the two isolators either side of it open. Only the section between them is lost. The rest is fed from both ends. EN 54-2 states that a single fault on a loop must not disable more than 32 detectors and/or call points. So designers place an isolator at least every 32 devices, and at every zone or fire compartment boundary.

The panel and its power

The fire panel (CIE) has its own certified power supply (EN 54-4). Batteries must keep the system in standby for a specified time and then sound the alarm. Common national rules are 24 h or 72 h standby plus 30 min of alarm. The longer figure applies where no service can reach site quickly.

Battery sizing is simple arithmetic. A panel with loops draws 0.45 A in standby and 1.6 A in alarm. For 72 h + 0.5 h: 0.45 × 72 + 1.6 × 0.5 = 33.2 Ah. Add an ageing factor of about 1.25 and you need around 42 Ah, so a pair of 12 V 45 Ah batteries.

Panels are networked with each other over a vendor ring (often fibre or redundant copper) so a fire anywhere shows on every panel and on the fire brigade panel. Examples: Siemens Cerberus PRO / Sinteso, Honeywell Esser, Bosch AVENAR, Schrack Integral, Polon-Alfa Polon 6000. Their loop protocols are proprietary. You cannot mix detectors across brands on the same loop.

A four-storey office has one panel with four loops. Each loop runs through two floors, about 110 devices on 1.4 km of red fire-resistant cable. An isolator sits at each floor boundary and inside every fifth detector base, so a fault never removes more than about 10 devices. A short caused by a fit-out contractor drilling into the cable on floor 3 took out 6 detectors. The panel showed "Loop 2 short circuit, between addresses 071 and 076" and the rest of the floor stayed protected.
A conventional zone has no isolators. A short on a conventional zone disables every detector on it. If you inherit conventional zones covering large areas, that is a real weakness, not just an old technology.

A single open circuit appears on a correctly wired addressable loop. What happens?

  • Addressable loops return to the panel and are fed from both ends. One break loses nothing.
  • Short-circuit isolators limit a short to one section, with no more than 32 devices lost (EN 54-2).
  • EN 54 parts certify each component; national codes decide the design.
  • Batteries size from standby current × hours plus alarm current × 30 min, plus an ageing margin.

16.2 Control matrix and fire scenarios

Detecting a fire is half the job. The other half is what happens next. That is defined in the fire scenario (Polish: scenariusz pożarowy) and implemented as a cause-and-effect matrix (matryca sterowań). Rows are causes: detection zones, call points, sprinkler flow switches. Columns are effects: sounders, door releases, dampers, lifts, fans, fire brigade transmission.

The scenario is written by the fire safety designer, agreed with the authority and the fire brigade, and then programmed into the panel by the fire system integrator. The BMS integrator reads it. They do not write it.

Try it: trigger a cause

Two-stage alarm and investigation time

Many buildings with staff on site use a two-stage alarm. A single automatic detector gives a stage I alarm: the panel buzzes at the security desk, nothing in the building sounds yet. Staff have a short time T1 to acknowledge, then a longer time T2 to check the room. If they confirm, or the timer runs out, or a second detector or a call point fires, the panel goes to stage II: general alarm, fire brigade transmission, all controls. Typical figures are T1 of 30 s to 2 min and T2 of a few minutes. The exact values are set in the scenario. A manual call point usually skips straight to stage II.

Coincidence (dependency) detection

For effects that are costly or dangerous when false, such as releasing a gas extinguishing agent, the scenario requires two independent detectors in the same area before acting. EN 54-2 calls these dependency types A, B and C. Type B, for example, waits for a second detector before triggering the effect.

A hotel scenario, simplified: a detector in a room gives stage I at reception. If it is confirmed, the floor and the floor above get the evacuation message, the staircase pressurisation fans start, lifts return to the ground floor, holding magnets on corridor doors release, and the AHU for that floor stops. A sprinkler flow switch skips stage I because water flow is already strong evidence.
The matrix is a legal document. A change to "just one output" requires the scenario author to agree, the panel to be reprogrammed by an authorised integrator, and the change to be tested and recorded. Never re-create fire logic in the BMS "because it is easier to edit there".

Staff acknowledge a stage I alarm but do nothing more. What happens when T2 runs out?

  • The fire scenario is a design document; the cause-and-effect matrix is its implementation in the panel.
  • Two-stage alarms trade a short delay (T1 + T2) for far fewer false evacuations.
  • Call points and sprinkler flow usually go straight to stage II.
  • Coincidence detection protects costly effects like gas release.

16.3 Smoke control and HVAC fire mode

Smoke kills more people than flames. Smoke control has two goals: keep escape routes clear and stop smoke spreading between fire compartments. It uses three tools.

ToolHowStandard (EU)
Fire dampersClose where a duct crosses a fire compartment wall. Thermal fuse (about 72 °C) or a spring-return actuator commanded by the panel.EN 15650, classified per EN 13501-3
Smoke extractionFans and smoke dampers pull smoke out of the fire zone; natural vents open in the roof. Fans rated e.g. F400/120 (400 °C for 120 min).EN 12101-3, -2, -8
Pressure differentialFans push fresh air into staircases or lobbies so smoke cannot enter when a door opens.EN 12101-6 (now EN 12101-13)

The ordinary HVAC must cooperate. An AHU that keeps recirculating air would spread smoke through the building. So the fire panel sends the AHU a fire signal, usually a hard-wired volt-free contact into the AHU safety chain. What the AHU then does is part of the scenario: often stop and close its dampers, sometimes switch to 100 % exhaust in the fire zone.

Fire mode sequence

Try it: staircase door force

Pressurising a staircase has a limit. Too much pressure and a child or an elderly person cannot open the door into the staircase. EN 12101-6 caps the opening force at 100 N. A common design pressure is around 50 Pa. The force needed at the handle is:

F = Fcloser + (W · A · ΔP) / (2 · (W − d)), where W is door width, A door area, ΔP the pressure difference and d the distance from the handle to the door edge.

Real pressurisation systems control the pressure. A relief damper or a variable-speed fan holds ΔP near setpoint when doors close, and pushes more air when a door opens, so air flows out of the staircase at, for example, 0.75 to 2 m/s depending on the system class.

An AHU serves three floors. On a confirmed alarm on floor 2, the fire panel opens a volt-free contact in the AHU control panel. The AHU controller stops both fans in its safety chain, closes the outdoor and exhaust air dampers and reports "fire stop" to the BMS. Fire dampers on floor 2 close from their own panel-commanded actuators. Fire dampers on the other floors stay open because their scenario says so. The BMS shows all of this. It commands none of it.
Fire-mode inputs must be hard-wired and fail safe. If the AHU controller only receives "fire" over BACnet from the BMS, a crashed server or a cut network cable means smoke gets pumped around the building. Hard-wired contact, normally closed, opening on alarm.

Why is the fire signal to an AHU usually a normally closed contact that opens on alarm?

  • Fire dampers block spread across compartments; smoke extraction removes smoke; pressurisation keeps stairs clear.
  • The AHU gets a hard-wired, fail-safe fire signal from the panel.
  • Staircase pressure is a balance: enough to stop smoke, low enough for 100 N door force.

16.4 Voice evacuation (DSO)

A sounder says "something is wrong". A spoken message says what to do. Studies of evacuations show people react faster and more correctly to clear speech than to bells. That is why larger public buildings use a voice alarm system (VA, or PAVA when it also does public address). In Poland this is DSO (dźwiękowy system ostrzegawczy).

ElementRoleStandard
VA controllerStores messages, routes them to zones, monitors lines and amplifiersEN 54-16
LoudspeakersFire-rated, often with ceramic terminal block and thermal fuseEN 54-24
Fire microphoneLive announcements by the fire brigade or staff, highest priorityEN 54-16
Power supplyBatteries for standby plus alarm timeEN 54-4
System designCoverage, intelligibility, zoningEN 50849, CEN/TS 54-32, national rules

The fire panel triggers the VA system over monitored contacts or a certified interface. The VA controller plays pre-recorded messages per zone. It also monitors every loudspeaker line (impedance or end-of-line module), every amplifier and the spare amplifier that takes over if one fails.

Intelligibility

Loud is not enough. Speech must be understood. It is measured as STI (Speech Transmission Index, 0 to 1). Common targets: average STI of at least 0.50, and no area below 0.45. Hard surfaces and long reverberation (a stone atrium, a parking garage) destroy intelligibility. The fix is more speakers at lower level, closer to listeners, not fewer louder ones. The sound level should also sit about 10 dB or more above the ambient noise, without being painfully loud.

Try it: phased evacuation

Tall buildings rarely evacuate everyone at once. Staircases would jam. Instead they use phased evacuation: the fire floor and the floor above get the "evacuate" message. Other floors get an "alert" message telling them to wait for instructions. After a set time, or by fire brigade decision, more floors are evacuated.

A 12-storey office uses a VA system with one zone per floor plus stairs and the lobby. Messages are recorded in two languages. The evacuation message starts with an attention tone, then "Attention please. An emergency has been reported in the building. Please leave by the nearest exit. Do not use the lifts." The alert message tells people to stay where they are and listen. Products used for systems like this include Bosch PRAESENSA, TOA VX-3000, Honeywell VARIODYN and Ambient Systems.
The VA system is a life-safety system even though it can also play background music. A general audio or AV-over-IP system (Chapter 14) is not a voice alarm unless it is certified as one. Never route evacuation messages through an ordinary DSP and network amplifiers and call it compliant.

An atrium measures STI 0.38 with the existing speakers. What usually helps most?

  • Spoken messages make people act faster than sounders.
  • VA systems are EN 54-16/-24 certified, with monitored lines and spare amplifiers.
  • Intelligibility (STI ≥ 0.50 typical) matters more than loudness.
  • Phased evacuation: evacuate fire floor and above, alert the rest.

16.5 BMS role: monitor, never override

The rule is short. The BMS may watch the fire system. It must not control it, and it must not be in the path of any fire-safety function. Fire systems are certified as a whole. Any device that can inhibit, delay or reset them becomes part of that certified system, which a BMS is not.

How the BMS sees fire data

MethodWhat the BMS getsNotes
Volt-free relay outputsFire alarm, fault, disablement (3 to a few contacts)Simplest, very robust, no detail
Vendor gateway to BACnet / ModbusPer-zone and per-device states, faults, analog valuesConfigured read-only. Examples: Siemens, Esser and Bosch offer BACnet or OPC gateways.

The boundary

Sort it: may the BMS do this?

Show fire alarm and fault states on floor plans
Reset the fire panel from the operator UI
Send a message to the facility manager on a fire fault
Be the only path that stops the AHU in fire
Restart the AHU normally after the fire signal clears
Disable a detector zone during cleaning work
Report how many false alarms each zone produced this year
Close a smoke damper again because the room got cold
A BACnet gateway on a networked fire system exposes about 900 objects: one Binary Input per zone for alarm and one for fault, Multi-State Inputs per device, and panel-level points. The gateway is configured read-only, so any write request is refused. The BMS shows a red floor-plan zone, sends a message to the facility manager, and logs the event. The certified transmission to the fire brigade runs over its own monitored path.
Some gateways can technically accept writes (silence, reset, disable). Turn this off. If the authority finds a BMS screen that can reset the fire panel, the system is no longer as certified, and after an incident the liability question becomes very simple and very unpleasant.

The BMS server crashes during a fire. What should be affected?

  • The BMS reads fire states through relay contacts or a read-only gateway.
  • No reset, silence, disable or override from the BMS. Ever.
  • Every fire function must work with the BMS and the network dead.

16.6 Regulations and periodic inspections

A fire system is only as good as its last test. Detectors get dirty, batteries age, a fit-out moves a wall and leaves a room without a detector. That is why inspection is a legal duty for the building owner, not a courtesy of the installer.

The details differ by country, but the shape is similar. Below is a generic pattern, with typical numbers from European practice. Always check the national regulation and the manufacturer's manual.

A typical inspection rhythm

  • DailyPanel checkNormal state, no faults or disablements. Done by the owner's staff.
  • WeeklyUser testOne call point per week in rotation, sounders heard. Logged.
  • QuarterlyService visitA sample of detectors, batteries, transmission to the brigade.
  • YearlyFull inspectionEvery device functionally tested by certified staff. Protocol issued.
  • Every few yearsSystem cooperation testFull scenario run with all linked systems. Batteries replaced on age.

In Poland the fire protection regulation requires inspection and maintenance of fire protection equipment at least once a year, by the manufacturer's rules. Recent amendments also require periodic tests of cooperation (próby współdziałania) between the systems in the scenario. VRLA batteries usually need replacing after about 4 to 5 years. Smoke detectors are typically cleaned or replaced on a cycle the manufacturer sets.

Try it: plan the yearly testing

The cooperation test

Individual device tests do not prove the scenario works. The cooperation test does: trigger real causes and watch every effect in the matrix happen, with timing. Fire dampers close, the AHU stops, lifts go down, doors release, VA plays the right message in the right zones, the alarm reaches the brigade. The BMS integrator is usually present to confirm the BMS shows the right states and does not interfere.

RecordWhy it matters
System log bookEvery alarm, fault, disablement, test and repair, with dates and names
Inspection protocolProof the yearly duty was met; asked for by inspectors and insurers
As-built scenario + matrixThe reference every test is measured against
False alarm statisticsTrends show bad detector choice or placement
A shopping centre with 2400 detectors has quarterly visits. Each visit tests 600 detectors with a telescopic pole, smoke aerosol and a heat tool, and a quarter of the call points. The panel's test mode groups results so the engineer gets a list of "devices not yet tested this year". In the fourth visit, the team also runs the cooperation test overnight, with the shops closed.
Testing puts zones into test or disable mode. Forgotten disablements are a classic cause of a fire going undetected. The daily panel check exists to catch them, and a BMS alarm on "fire system disablement active for more than 8 h" is a valuable, read-only extra.

Which test proves that the cause-and-effect matrix actually works end to end?

  • Daily checks by staff, periodic visits by certified technicians, a full yearly inspection.
  • Cooperation tests prove the scenario across all linked systems.
  • Keep the log book, protocols and as-built matrix up to date.
  • Watch for forgotten disablements.
