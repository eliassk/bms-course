Chapter 21

Alarm management

An alarm is a request for a human to act. Everything in this chapter follows from that one sentence: which conditions deserve an alarm, how urgent each one is, how to stop noise, how to pair "raised" with "cleared", which channel reaches a person, and what happens when nobody answers.

21.1 Alarm philosophy (ISA-18.2, EEMUA 191)

Most building systems are born with too many alarms. Every controller vendor ships hundreds of possible alarm points, integrators enable them "to be safe", and within a year the operators have learned to ignore the alarm list. Alarm management is the discipline that prevents this. It came from the process industry after accidents where operators were buried in alarms, and two documents define it:

DocumentWhat it isWhat to take from it
EEMUA 191UK industry guide, first published 1999 after incidents such as the 1994 Milford Haven refinery explosion. Now in its third edition.Practical benchmarks: alarm rates, priority distribution, the "alarm must be useful and relevant" test.
ANSI/ISA-18.2US standard (2009, revised 2016) for alarm management in process industries.The alarm lifecycle, formal alarm states, the definition of an alarm, required documents.
IEC 62682International standard closely based on ISA-18.2.Same model, cited in European tenders.

Buildings are not refineries. Nobody dies if a meeting room is 2 K too warm. But the human problem is identical: one person, a phone, and a stream of messages. The principles scale down very well.

The definition that does the work

ISA-18.2 defines an alarm as an audible and/or visible means of indicating an equipment malfunction, process deviation or abnormal condition requiring a response. Read the last three words twice. A message that requires no action is not an alarm. It is an event, a log entry or a status. That gives you a simple test for every candidate alarm:

  1. Is there a defined action? "Call the boiler contractor", "reset the pump", "close the window". If you cannot write the action, it is not an alarm.
  2. Is it unique? If another alarm already tells the operator the same thing, one of them goes.
  3. Is it timely? It must arrive early enough for the action to matter, and not so early it is noise.
  4. Is it understandable? "AHU2 supply fan failed, building west wing unventilated" beats "DI 4.07 = 1".

The lifecycle

ISA-18.2 describes alarm management as a loop, not a one-off configuration task. Click a stage.

The philosophy document

The first stage produces a short alarm philosophy. For a building it can be four pages. It fixes the rules before anyone configures a single point:

  • What qualifies as an alarm, and what is only an event.
  • Priority levels and how to pick one (the matrix in 21.2).
  • Target rates: average alarms per operator per hour, and what counts as a flood.
  • Who may shelve or disable an alarm, for how long, and how it is logged.
  • Notification channels per priority, on-call rules, escalation.
  • Naming and message text conventions.

Benchmarks: how many is too many?

EEMUA 191 and ISA-18.2 give rough targets for one operator. ISA-18.2 treats about 150 alarms per day (about one per 10 minutes) as very likely acceptable and about 300 per day (two per 10 minutes) as maximum manageable. A flood starts when more than 10 alarms arrive in 10 minutes. These numbers come from control rooms with a dedicated operator. A building technician who also fixes things should aim far lower: a handful per day.

A typical "power blip" flood: the mains dips for 300 ms. Every VFD trips, every room controller reboots and reports a communication loss, the UPS reports "on battery", and the BMS logs 200 alarms in 2 minutes. The only actionable one is "AHU 1 did not restart". Good design suppresses the consequential alarms while "mains failure" is active (state-based suppression, see 21.3) and leaves one root cause on screen.
Measuring is the step most sites skip. Without a weekly "top 10 most frequent alarms" report, nobody notices the one sensor that produced 60 % of all alarms last month. In practice a few bad actors usually dominate the count.

A point raises "Filter pressure logged: 142 Pa" every hour. No action is defined. What is it under ISA-18.2?

  • An alarm requires a response. No action, no alarm.
  • ISA-18.2 / IEC 62682 give the lifecycle and states; EEMUA 191 gives the benchmarks.
  • ~1 alarm per 10 min is acceptable for a full-time operator; >10 per 10 min is a flood.
  • Write a short philosophy first. Measure every week after.

21.2 Severity, priority, alarm fatigue

Two words are often mixed up. Severity describes how bad the consequence is if nothing is done. Priority tells the operator what to handle first. Priority is derived from severity and from how much time there is to respond. A leak into the server room is severe and fast. A failed backup boiler in July is severe on paper but there are months to fix it.

The priority matrix

During rationalisation, each alarm is placed on a matrix. Pick the worst credible consequence and the maximum time before the consequence happens. The cell gives the priority.

PriorityTarget shareBuilding exampleExpected response
High / critical~5 %Water leak in plant room, freezer above −10 °C, fire panel faultMinutes, any hour
Medium~15 %Boiler lockout in winter, AHU fan failed, UPS on batteryWithin an hour
Low~80 %Filter dirty, sensor out of range, door held openNext working day

The 80/15/5 split is the EEMUA-style guideline. If 40 % of your alarms are "critical", the word means nothing. Three or four priority levels are enough; more levels cannot be told apart under stress.

Alarm fatigue

Alarm fatigue is the learned habit of ignoring alarms because most of them did not matter. It is well studied in hospitals, where a large share of bedside monitor alarms are false or clinically irrelevant. The mechanism is simple conditioning: if 19 out of 20 messages are noise, the brain stops treating the 20th as a signal. The slider below is a toy model, not measured data. It shows the shape of the problem.

Do not "fix" fatigue by making everything high priority. That trains the same habit at a louder volume. Fix it by deleting, merging and tuning alarms until nearly every one leads to an action.
A cold-store alarm is set at −15 °C. Each defrost cycle lifts the air probe to −12 °C for 20 minutes, four times a day. Staff learn to ignore it. One night the compressor fails and nobody reacts for hours. The fix: suppress during the defrost window, use the product probe instead of the air probe, and add a 30-minute delay.

A backup boiler fails in July. The consequence (no heat) is severe, but heating is not needed for months. What priority?

  • Severity = how bad. Priority = what first. Priority uses severity and time to respond.
  • Aim for roughly 80 % low, 15 % medium, 5 % high.
  • Fatigue comes from noise. Cure it by removing noise, not by shouting louder.

21.3 Debounce, cooldown, shelving

A real signal is noisy. A temperature hovers around its limit, a float switch bounces as water ripples, a network link drops for two seconds. Without filtering, each crossing becomes an alarm. An alarm that raises and clears repeatedly in a short time is called chattering. These tools stop it:

ToolWhat it doesTypical values
Deadband (hysteresis)Raise at the limit, clear only after the value is back by a margin.Temperature 0.5–2 K; pressure 2–5 % of range
On-delay (debounce)The condition must hold for N seconds before the alarm raises.Digital inputs 1–5 s; temperatures 5–15 min; comm loss 30–120 s
Off-delayThe condition must be gone for N seconds before the alarm clears.Similar to on-delay or shorter
Cooldown (re-notify limit)After one notification, do not send another for the same alarm for N minutes.15–60 min
ShelvingAn operator hides a known nuisance alarm for a fixed time. It returns automatically.1 h to 1 shift; max set in philosophy
Suppression by designLogic hides alarms that are expected in a state (plant off, maintenance mode, mains failure).Rule-based, no timer
Out of serviceAlarm removed for maintenance, with a permit and a log entry.Days, with review

Try it

A room temperature with noise and one real fault (heating failure around minute 140). The alarm limit is below the setpoint: low temperature. Tune the filters to get one alarm for the real fault and none for noise.

Notice the trade-off. Every second of on-delay is a second of later warning for the real fault. For a slow room temperature, 10 minutes costs nothing. For a water leak, a 10-minute delay is a flooded floor. Pick delays per alarm, from the time-to-respond column of the matrix.

Implementation sketch

// Evaluated on every new sample.
const low = value < limit;
const clear = value >= limit + deadband;
if (!active && low)   { since ??= now; if (now - since >= onDelay)  raise(); }
else if (active && clear) { since ??= now; if (now - since >= offDelay) clear_(); }
else since = null;           // Condition broke; restart the timer.

Two details matter. The timer resets when the condition breaks, so a bouncing signal never satisfies the delay. And timers MUST survive a restart of the alarm engine, or a reboot raises every pending alarm at once.

Shelving rules

  • Shelving always has an end time. "Shelved forever" is a disabled alarm without paperwork.
  • Record who shelved it, when and why.
  • Show shelved alarms in a separate list, so the shift handover sees them.
  • Do not allow high-priority alarms to be shelved without a second person, if your philosophy says so.
A cooldown limits notifications, not the alarm state. The alarm list must still show the current truth. A common bug: the cooldown blocks the "cleared" message too, so the phone says "leak!" and never says "dry again".

A low-temperature alarm at 19.5 °C chatters as the room sits at 19.4–19.6 °C. What is the cleanest single fix?

  • Deadband stops chatter at the limit; on-delay stops short spikes.
  • Delays cost warning time. Choose them per alarm.
  • Shelving is temporary, logged and visible. Suppression by design handles expected states.
  • Cooldown throttles messages, never the alarm state itself.

21.4 Raise and clear pairs

An alarm is a state, not a message. It has a beginning (raised), an end (cleared, or return to normal) and a human side (acknowledged or not). If you only send a message when something goes wrong, the receiver never learns that it is over. They either drive to site for nothing or stop trusting the system.

Two independent bits

ISA-18.2 models an alarm as two dimensions: is the process abnormal now, and has a person acknowledged it. That gives four basic states:

StateProcessAcknowledgedTypical display
NormalOKyesNot shown
UnacknowledgedAbnormalnoFlashing, with sound
AcknowledgedAbnormalyesSteady, no sound
RTN unacknowledgedOKnoShown dimmed until acknowledged (some systems clear it automatically)

Plus the special states: shelved, suppressed by design and out of service. Drive the state machine yourself:

Designing the pair

  • One alarm ID for both messages, so the receiver (and the log) can match them: ahu2.fan_fail, not two free-text strings.
  • Same text root: "AHU2 supply fan failed" / "Cleared: AHU2 supply fan running again (down 43 min)". Including the duration is cheap and very useful.
  • Clear goes to the same people who got the raise, on a quiet channel. Waking someone at 3 a.m. to say "it is fine now" is its own nuisance; a chat message is enough.
  • Clear after restart: after a reboot the engine MUST re-read the real state. A raise sent before the reboot needs a clear even if the engine "forgot".
  • Unknown is not normal: if a sensor goes offline, the alarm is neither raised nor cleared. Raise a separate "communication lost" alarm and keep the original state as unknown.
Message pair from a well-run site. 02:14 "HIGH: Plant room B leak sensor wet. Action: isolate valve V-B1, call on-call plumber." 02:51 "Cleared: Plant room B leak sensor dry (wet 37 min). Acknowledged by duty engineer at 02:19." The second message tells everyone the event is closed and who handled it.
Edge vs level. If the notifier triggers on changes only (edge), a missed MQTT or webhook message means the clear never arrives. Periodically reconcile against the current level: every few minutes, compare the list of active alarms with the real states and repair the difference.

An alarm is acknowledged, then the condition goes away. Which state is it in now?

  • An alarm is a state with two bits: process abnormal, person acknowledged.
  • Every raise gets a matching clear, with the same ID and the duration.
  • Reconcile against real state; do not trust edges alone. Unknown is not normal.

21.5 Notification channels: Telegram, SMS, push, e-mail

Most building alarms are not watched in a control room. They go to a phone. Each channel has different reliability, cost, speed and loudness. A good design routes by priority, not "everything everywhere".

ChannelHow it worksStrengthsWeaknesses
Chat bot (Telegram, Slack, Teams, Signal)HTTPS API call to the provider; the app delivers to a chat or group.Free or cheap, rich text, groups, buttons for "acknowledge", history.Needs internet on both ends; muted by many users; provider rate limits (Telegram documents roughly 1 message per second per chat and lower limits for groups).
SMSHTTP API to an SMS gateway (Twilio, MessageBird, local operators) or a GSM modem on site.Works without mobile data; a GSM modem works without internet; loud by default.Costs per message; 160 GSM-7 characters per segment; no delivery guarantee; some delays; no rich content.
Mobile pushYour app's server sends to APNs (Apple) or FCM (Google), which deliver to the device.Free, instant, can carry buttons; iOS "critical alerts" can break through silent mode (needs a special Apple entitlement).Needs your own app; OS battery savers may delay; tokens expire.
E-mailSMTP via a relay or provider API.Free, good for reports and low-priority items, auditable.Slow to be noticed; spam filters (set up SPF, DKIM, DMARC); never for urgent alarms.
Voice callTelephony API reads text aloud and asks for a key press.Hardest to ignore; key press = acknowledgement.Costs most; annoying if overused.

Route by priority

SMS: count your segments

SMS is billed per segment. With the GSM-7 alphabet one segment holds 160 characters, or 153 each when the message is split. A single character outside GSM-7, for example "ł", "ő" or an emoji, switches the whole message to UCS-2: 70 characters, or 67 per part. Some gateways transliterate automatically; many do not.

Message content

A mobile alarm is read on a lock screen. The first 40 characters decide whether it gets opened. Put priority, place and problem first, the action second, and the link or ID last:

HIGH · Plant room B · Leak sensor wet
Isolate valve V-B1. Call on-call plumber.
ID leak.prb.1 · 02:14 · reply ACK
Every channel depends on something. Chat bots and push need internet; a hosted SMS API needs internet too. If the alarm that matters most is "internet down" or "power failed", only a channel with a separate path can tell you: a GSM modem on UPS, or an external watchdog that alarms when your system stops sending heartbeats ("dead man's switch").
A common pattern: a team group chat for all medium and low alarms and every clear; SMS to the on-call phone for high alarms; a daily e-mail digest of everything still open; and an external uptime service that expects a heartbeat every 5 minutes and sends its own SMS when it stops.

Which channel can still report "site internet is down" from the site itself?

  • Route by priority: loud channels for few alarms, quiet ones for the rest and for clears.
  • SMS: 160 GSM-7 or 70 UCS-2 characters per segment. One emoji changes the bill.
  • Have one path that does not share the failure you are alarming on, plus an external heartbeat watchdog.

21.6 Escalation and on-call

A notification that nobody reads is the same as no notification. Escalation closes that loop: if an alarm is not acknowledged within a set time, the system tries the next person or a louder channel. Tools such as PagerDuty, Opsgenie or Grafana OnCall do this for IT; many BMS platforms and simple scripts can do it too.

Building blocks

ConceptMeaning
On-call scheduleWho is responsible now. Weekly rotation is common; handover at a fixed time, for example Monday 09:00.
Escalation policyOrdered steps: notify A; after N minutes without ack, notify A louder and B; then the manager.
Acknowledgement"I own this." Stops escalation. Not the same as fixed.
ResolutionThe condition clears (21.4). Closes the incident.
Re-alertIf acknowledged but still active after a long time (for example 2 h), notify again.

Choosing timeouts

Timeouts come from the matrix again. If a leak must be handled within 30 minutes, three steps of 10 minutes is already the whole budget, and the last step reaches someone with no time left. Shorter steps for high priority (5 minutes), longer for medium (15–30 minutes), no escalation for low priority: those go to the next-day list.

Human rules

  • Acknowledging MUST be one tap: a button in the chat, a reply to the SMS, a key press on the call.
  • Everyone who received the alarm should see who acknowledged it, so two people do not drive to site.
  • Test the whole chain on a schedule, for example a monthly test alarm that must be acknowledged.
  • Count night pages per on-call week. If someone is woken more than a couple of times a week by things that could wait, fix the alarms, not the person.
  • Respect working-time and on-call agreements; escalation design is also an HR question.
A typical small-site policy for high-priority alarms: step 1 at 0 min, chat message and SMS to the on-call technician; step 2 at 10 min, voice call to the technician and SMS to the backup; step 3 at 20 min, call to the facility manager. Medium alarms only send step 1 and re-notify once after 60 minutes.
The escalation engine is itself a single point of failure. If it runs on the same server as the BMS and that server dies, nothing escalates. Monitor it from outside with a heartbeat.

The on-call technician acknowledges an alarm but the leak is still active. What should happen?

  • Escalate on missing acknowledgement, in timed steps, to more people or louder channels.
  • Ack = ownership, clear = resolution. Different events.
  • Timeouts come from time-to-respond. Test the chain regularly and watch it from outside.
