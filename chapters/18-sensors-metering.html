Chapter 18

Sensors and metering

Every control loop, alarm and energy report starts with a number from a sensor or a meter. This chapter looks at how those numbers are produced, how they go wrong, and how to make them trustworthy: resistance thermometers, air quality sensors, leak and gas detection, energy meters, placement, drift and battery-powered wireless devices.

18.1 Temperature sensors: NTC, PT1000, digital

Temperature is the most common measurement in a building. A medium office has hundreds of points: rooms, supply and return water, supply and extract air, outdoor air, domestic hot water. Almost all of them use one of three families.

The RTD equation

Platinum RTDs follow the Callendar–Van Dusen equation. For 0 °C and above it is R(t) = R0 · (1 + A·t + B·t²) with A = 3.9083·10⁻³ and B = −5.775·10⁻⁷. R0 is 100 Ω for a PT100 and 1000 Ω for a PT1000. The PT1000 is not more accurate than a PT100. It just produces ten times more ohms per kelvin, so the same cable resistance causes one tenth of the error. That is why building automation uses PT1000 and Ni1000 almost everywhere, while process plants with 4-wire transmitters still use PT100.

Accuracy is set by tolerance class. IEC 60751 class B allows ±(0.30 + 0.005·|t|) K, so ±0.3 K at 0 °C and ±0.55 K at 50 °C. Class A is ±(0.15 + 0.002·|t|) K. Heat meters use matched pairs, because there the difference between two sensors matters more than either absolute value.

The NTC equation

An NTC is usually described by its B value: R(T) = R25 · exp(B · (1/T − 1/298.15)) with T in kelvin. The steep curve gives a big signal near room temperature, but sensitivity collapses at high temperatures. A 10 kΩ NTC drops to a few hundred ohms above 100 °C, where a small resistance error becomes a large temperature error. Controllers ship a table per sensor type. Choosing the wrong type in configuration is a classic commissioning mistake: a "10k3" read as a "10k2" can be off by a degree or more away from 25 °C.

Lead resistance: 2, 3 and 4 wires

A resistance sensor cannot tell its own ohms from the ohms of the cable. Copper has a resistivity of about 0.0175 Ω·mm²/m. A 50 m run of 0.5 mm² wire has 1.75 Ω per conductor, 3.5 Ω for the loop. On a PT100 that is 9 K of error. On a PT1000 it is 0.9 K. On an NTC near room temperature it is less than 0.01 K.

  • 2-wire: the input sees sensor plus both leads. Fine for PT1000 and NTC on short runs, and many controllers allow a fixed offset to compensate.
  • 3-wire: a third lead lets the input measure one lead and subtract it, assuming both leads are equal. The residual error comes from the mismatch between leads and from terminals.
  • 4-wire: a constant current flows through two leads and the voltage is sensed on two others that carry almost no current. Lead resistance drops out completely.

Digital sensors

A digital sensor moves the analog problem into the chip. A DS18B20 on a 1-Wire bus gives ±0.5 °C between −10 and +85 °C and needs no calibration table. A Sensirion SHT4x reports temperature and humidity over I²C. Room units then speak Modbus RTU, BACnet MS/TP, KNX or a radio protocol. The trade-off moves: no cable errors, but bus topology, addressing, firmware and power supply become your problem. A frozen digital value looks exactly like a stable room, so the BMS should watch for values that never change (Chapter 21).

A supply water sensor on a district heating substation is a PT1000 class B in a brass immersion pocket, wired 2-wire with 15 m of 0.75 mm² cable. Lead error: about 0.7 Ω, or 0.18 K. The controller allows a 0.2 K offset, so the commissioning engineer checks it against a reference probe in the same pocket and trims it. Total error after trimming: well under 0.5 K, which is fine for a weather compensated loop.
Immersion sensors need thermal paste or oil in the pocket and full insertion depth. An air gap in a pocket can make a sensor read several kelvin low and respond in minutes instead of seconds. Strap-on sensors on uninsulated pipes read lower than the water inside.

A 2-wire PT100 on 60 m of 0.5 mm² cable reads about 11 K high. What is the best fix?

  • PT1000 and Ni1000 dominate building automation because their larger signal tolerates cable resistance.
  • NTCs are cheap and sensitive but come in many curves. The configured type MUST match the sensor.
  • 2-wire adds both leads, 3-wire subtracts one, 4-wire removes them.
  • Digital sensors remove analog cable error but add bus, firmware and stale-value risks.

18.2 Humidity, CO₂, pressure, flow

Humidity

Almost all building humidity sensors are capacitive: a thin polymer film absorbs water vapour and its dielectric constant changes. They report relative humidity (%RH), which depends strongly on temperature. Air at 21 °C and 50 %RH has a dew point near 10 °C. Warm the same air to 25 °C and it reads about 40 %RH, though no water was added. That is why good sensors put temperature and humidity on the same chip, and why the BMS often computes dew point or absolute humidity rather than controlling on %RH alone. Typical accuracy is ±2 to ±3 %RH in the middle of the range, worse above 80 %RH. Long exposure to condensing conditions or solvents causes drift.

CO₂

Indoor CO₂ comes mostly from people. Outdoor air is roughly 420 ppm. CO₂ is a proxy for how well a room is ventilated relative to its occupancy, not a toxic concern at normal indoor levels. Common design targets are 800 to 1200 ppm, depending on the standard and the class of indoor climate.

Many single-channel NDIR sensors use automatic baseline correction (ABC). They assume the lowest reading over a week or so is outdoor air, about 400 ppm, and shift the curve to match. This works in offices that empty at night. It quietly fails in rooms that are always occupied or always poorly ventilated, where the sensor drifts down and reports good air when it is not.

Try it: CO₂ in a meeting room

A seated adult breathes out about 0.005 L/s of CO₂. In a well-mixed room the concentration approaches a steady state C = C_out + G / Q, where G is the CO₂ generation and Q the outdoor air flow. The time constant is V / Q.

Pressure

HVAC pressure sensors are mostly differential: piezoresistive or capacitive membranes between two ports. Ranges are small. A filter alarm might trip at 150 to 250 Pa, a duct static pressure setpoint might be 250 Pa, a clean-room cascade only 10 to 15 Pa. Water-side sensors measure bar: system fill pressure (1 to 3 bar in a typical heating system) and differential pressure across pumps or valves. Low-range air sensors need periodic zeroing, and many have an auto-zero valve for that.

A room unit that "measures CO₂" for a low price may contain only a MOX VOC sensor with an eCO₂ estimate. Check the datasheet for NDIR or photoacoustic before using it for demand-controlled ventilation.

A hospital ward's NDIR CO₂ sensor slowly reads lower each month, though complaints rise. Most likely cause?

  • Relative humidity depends on temperature. Control on dew point or absolute humidity when it matters.
  • NDIR or photoacoustic for CO₂. ABC needs a room that regularly sees outdoor air.
  • HVAC differential pressures are tens to hundreds of pascals and need zeroing.
  • Pick flow meters by medium, conductivity, pipe size and low-flow behaviour.

18.3 Leak and gas sensors

Water leak detection

A water leak in a plant room, server room or under a kitchen is cheap to detect and expensive to miss. Leak sensors measure conductivity between electrodes. Water bridges them and current flows.

TypeCoverageOutputTypical place
Spot probeA few cm²Dry contact or radio messageLowest point under a boiler, drip tray, sump
Sensing cableUp to tens of metres per zoneContact; zoned controllersAround CRAC units, along pipe runs, under raised floors
Location sensing cableHundreds of metresDistance to the leak, e.g. "47 m"Data centres, museums, archives
Flow-based (meter logic)Whole supplyAlarm on continuous flowNight flow detection on the main water meter

A detector is half the answer. The other half is what happens next: a motorised shut-off valve on the zone supply, an alarm to the person who can act, and a test routine. Valves that never move tend to seize, so good designs exercise them, for example monthly.

Gas detection

Fixed gas detection protects people and plant in boiler rooms, kitchens, car parks and refrigeration plant. It is a safety function. The detection controller and its shut-off valve act locally. The BMS only monitors alarm, fault and possibly the gas level.

Sensing principleGasesRangeNotes

Flammable gases are measured as a percentage of the lower explosive limit (LEL). For methane the LEL is about 4.4 % by volume (older references use 5 %). Detectors typically raise a first alarm around 10 to 20 % LEL and a second, shut-off alarm around 20 to 40 % LEL. Exact values come from national rules and the risk assessment. Car park CO detection is typically in ppm, with thresholds in the tens of ppm used to stage extract fans.

Where to mount: it depends on density

A gas-fired boiler room has two catalytic or IR methane heads near the ceiling, above the burners and near the gas train. A gas detection controller drives a normally closed solenoid valve on the incoming gas line and cuts burner enable at the second alarm level. The BMS reads three dry contacts: pre-alarm, alarm and fault. It shows them, logs them and notifies staff, but it is not in the shut-off chain.
Catalytic sensors can be poisoned by silicone sealants and some cleaning sprays, losing sensitivity without any fault signal. Gas detectors need regular bump tests and calibration with test gas, often every 6 or 12 months, and a log of each test.

Where should an LPG detector be mounted?

  • Leak detection is only useful with an action: shut-off valve, alarm routing and tests.
  • Flammable gas is measured in % LEL, toxic gas in ppm.
  • Light gases are detected high, heavy gases low.
  • Gas detection is a local safety function. The BMS monitors it.

18.4 Energy, heat and water meters

A sensor feeds a control loop. A meter feeds a bill, a report or a contract. That changes the rules: meters are type-approved, sealed and accurate over years. In the EU, meters used for billing fall under the Measuring Instruments Directive (MID), which defines accuracy classes and marking.

Electricity meters

A modern electricity meter samples voltage and current and integrates power into energy. MID classes A, B and C correspond roughly to 2 %, 1 % and 0.5 % accuracy. Large or main meters use current transformers, and the CT ratio must be configured correctly. Getting it wrong scales every reading. Outputs include:

  • S0 pulse: a contact pulse per fixed energy, e.g. 1000 imp/kWh. Simple, robust, only energy.
  • Modbus RTU/TCP: energy, power, voltage, current, power factor per phase. The usual BMS route.
  • M-Bus or wireless M-Bus: standard for utility meters in Europe, read at intervals.

Heat meters

A heat (or cooling) meter combines three parts: a flow sensor, a matched pair of temperature sensors on supply and return, and a calculator. It integrates

Q = ∫ ṁ · c · (T_supply − T_return) dt

For water near normal heating temperatures, kW ≈ 1.16 · flow[m³/h] · ΔT[K]. The calculator uses temperature-dependent density and heat capacity, so the real factor varies slightly. Heat meters in Europe follow EN 1434. The weak point is the temperature difference. A pair error of 0.1 K is nothing when ΔT is 20 K. It is 2 % of the reading when ΔT is 5 K and 5 % at 2 K. That is why meters specify a minimum ΔT and why low-ΔT systems are hard to meter.

Reading pulse meters

With a pulse output the BMS counts pulses for energy and measures the time between pulses for power: P[kW] = 3600 / (imp_per_kWh · t[s]). At 1000 imp/kWh a 2 kW load gives one pulse every 1.8 s. A 50 W standby load gives one every 72 s, so the displayed power lags badly at low load. Counters must survive controller restarts, so store the total, not just the rate.

Water meters

Water meters under MID are described by Q3, the permanent flow (for example Q3 = 2.5 m³/h for a house), and a ratio R = Q3/Q1 such as R160, which tells how low a flow it still measures accurately. Mechanical meters often have a reed or inductive pulse pick-up. Ultrasonic meters measure lower flows and often add leak and backflow flags in their M-Bus telegram.

An office floor gets a heat meter with a DN20 ultrasonic flow sensor (qp 2.5 m³/h), two PT1000 sensors in pockets and an M-Bus calculator. The BMS polls it every 15 minutes through an M-Bus level converter. Typical winter values: 1.2 m³/h, 55/40 °C, so ΔT = 15 K and about 21 kW. In summer the floor sees only DHW circulation losses at ΔT 2 K, and the meter's relative error there is much larger.

A heat meter shows 1.0 m³/h and ΔT 3 K. The sensors have a 0.15 K pair error. Roughly how big is the power error?

  • Billing meters are type-approved (MID in the EU). Sensors are not.
  • Heat power is about 1.16 × m³/h × ΔT. Small ΔT means large relative error.
  • Pulses give energy well, power poorly at low load.
  • Check CT ratios, pulse weights and sensor positions at commissioning.

18.5 Placement, calibration, drift

A perfect sensor in the wrong place measures the wrong thing perfectly. Most room temperature complaints that turn out to be "sensor problems" are placement problems.

Room sensor placement

A room sensor should see air that represents the occupied zone. Common guidance: about 1.2 to 1.5 m above the floor, on an internal wall, away from direct sun, heat sources, doors, supply air jets and corners. Click the positions.

Plant sensor placement

  • Supply air: after the fan and after enough distance for mixing, not right behind a coil.
  • Mixed air: averaging sensors (a long capillary or several elements) across the duct, because layers of cold and warm air do not mix quickly.
  • Outdoor air: north face or shaded, away from exhaust air, windows and dark walls.
  • Water: immersion pockets in the flow, not in dead legs. Downstream of mixing valves by several pipe diameters.

Calibration and drift

Every sensor changes slowly with age, temperature cycling, contamination and humidity. The rate depends on the technology. PT1000 elements are very stable. Humidity sensors may drift in the order of 0.25 %RH per year in clean air and much faster in polluted or condensing air. Single-channel NDIR sensors age with their lamp unless ABC corrects them. Electrochemical gas cells lose sensitivity until they are replaced.

Calibration means comparing against a reference with known, better accuracy. Field practice has three levels: a check (portable reference next to the sensor, record the difference), an adjustment (offset or two-point correction in the controller or sensor) and replacement (cheaper than calibration for most room sensors).

A school has 40 classroom CO₂ sensors. During annual maintenance a technician carries a calibrated handheld NDIR meter, holds it next to each sensor for a few minutes with the room empty and windows open, and records both values. Sensors more than 75 ppm off are adjusted or replaced. The BMS also compares each sensor's nightly minimum against 420 ppm and flags any that settle far from it.
Analytics can catch drift that people miss: two sensors in the same duct disagreeing, a room never dropping below 600 ppm overnight, a supply temperature that never changes. Use them, but do not "fix" a sensor by offsetting it to match another sensor that may be the wrong one.

A room is always 2 K warmer than the sensor says, only on sunny afternoons. First suspect?

  • Placement errors are larger and more common than sensor errors.
  • Internal wall, about 1.5 m, away from sun, heat, doors and jets.
  • Drift depends on technology and environment. Plan checks, adjustments or replacements.
  • Use the BMS to spot drift with plausibility checks.

18.6 Wireless sensors and batteries

Wireless sensors make retrofits possible where cables are not: listed buildings, glass walls, rented offices. The cost is moved from installation to maintenance. Every battery must be replaced, and every radio link can fail.

Where the battery goes

A battery sensor sleeps most of the time at a few microamps. It wakes, measures, transmits a short burst at tens of milliamps, and sleeps again. The average current is

I_avg = I_sleep + I_tx · t_tx / interval

Life is roughly usable capacity divided by average current, limited by the battery's own self-discharge and by cold, which reduces capacity. Radio airtime dominates. A LoRaWAN uplink at spreading factor 7 lasts tens of milliseconds. At SF12 the same payload can take over a second, so a device far from its gateway can use more than ten times the energy per message.

Designing a wireless sensor estate

  • Report on change plus heartbeat. Send when the value moves by, say, 0.2 K, and at least every 15 to 60 minutes, so silence means "dead" rather than "unchanged".
  • Watch battery and link quality. Trend battery voltage, RSSI and SNR. Alarm on low battery weeks before failure, and on missed heartbeats.
  • Plan replacements in batches. Hundreds of sensors bought together die together. Replace by area during routine maintenance.
  • Mind the building. Concrete, metal lift shafts, foil-backed insulation and coated glass attenuate radio heavily. Survey before you buy.
A university retrofits 600 lecture-room sensors on LoRaWAN with 2 × AA lithium cells, reporting temperature, humidity and CO₂ every 10 minutes. Sensors near gateways run at SF7 and are estimated at over 8 years. A dozen in basements fall back to SF11 or SF12, and the operator adds one more gateway rather than replace those batteries every 2 to 3 years.
NDIR CO₂ sensors are power-hungry: the lamp and measurement take far more energy than the radio message. Battery CO₂ sensors therefore measure less often or use low-power photoacoustic modules. Check how often the sensor actually measures, not just how often it reports.

Which single change usually extends a LoRaWAN sensor's battery life the most?

  • Average current = sleep + transmit energy spread over the interval.
  • Airtime, interval and retries dominate battery life.
  • Report on change plus heartbeat, and alarm on silence and low battery.
  • Energy harvesting (EnOcean) removes batteries for simple devices.
