Chapter 11

Ventilation and air handling

Why buildings need fresh air, how an air handling unit treats it, how heat is recovered from the exhaust, and how fans, dampers and sensors are controlled so the right amount of air arrives at the right condition for the least energy.

11.1 Indoor air quality: CO₂, humidity, VOC

People indoors produce heat, moisture, CO₂, odours and particles. Buildings add their own emissions: furniture, paint, cleaning products, printers. Ventilation dilutes all of it with outdoor air. The question a controller must answer is simple: how much outdoor air, right now? To answer it, it needs an indicator.

CO₂ as a proxy for people

CO₂ at indoor levels is not the main health problem. It is used because it tracks human occupancy very well and is cheap to measure. A seated adult exhales about 0.005 L/s of CO₂ (roughly 18 L/h). Outdoor air is about 420 ppm today. If the room is well mixed, the steady-state concentration is:

C = Cout + n × G ÷ Q × 10⁶

with n people, G = 0.005 L/s each and Q the outdoor airflow in L/s. Ten people with 100 L/s (10 L/s per person) settle at 420 + 500 = 920 ppm. With 5 L/s per person it is 1 420 ppm. The volume of the room does not change where you end up. It only changes how fast you get there.

The time constant is τ = V ÷ Q. A 120 m³ room with 120 L/s (0.12 m³/s) has τ = 1 000 s, about 17 minutes. After one τ you are 63 % of the way to steady state, after three τ about 95 %. Short meetings in big rooms may never reach steady state, which is why a fixed airflow sized for full occupancy is often wasted.

Humidity

Relative humidity (RH) between about 30 % and 60 % is the usual comfort band. Below ~30 % eyes and airways dry out and static builds up. Above ~60 % for long periods, mould and dust mites thrive. In cold climates, ventilation dries buildings in winter: −10 °C outdoor air at 80 % RH holds about 1.4 g/kg of water. Warmed to 21 °C, that is under 10 % RH. Section 11.7 explains why.

VOC and particles

VOC sensors (metal-oxide, MOX) react to a broad mix of gases: solvents, cleaning products, cooking, body odour. They do not give a true concentration of any one gas. They usually output a relative "air quality index" or a "CO₂-equivalent" estimate, and they self-calibrate against the cleanest air they have seen recently. They are good for rooms where people are not the main source: toilets, kitchens, print rooms. PM sensors (optical, PM2.5/PM10) watch particles, mostly from outdoors or from cooking.

Many cheap NDIR sensors use "automatic baseline correction": they assume the lowest reading in a week is 400 ppm. In a room that is occupied 24/7 (a hospital ward, a server room with staff) that assumption is false and the sensor drifts low. Dual-channel NDIR sensors avoid this. Check the datasheet.
A 60-seat lecture room is designed for 8 L/s per person, 480 L/s. On a typical day it holds 15 students. With CO₂ control at 900 ppm the supply drops to around 150 L/s. Fan power falls by much more than that, because power scales with the cube of speed (section 11.4).

20 people, 200 L/s outdoor air, outdoor 420 ppm. What is the approximate steady-state CO₂?

  • CO₂ is a proxy for people. Steady state = outdoor + n·G/Q. Volume only sets the speed (τ = V/Q).
  • 10 L/s per person gives roughly 500 ppm above outdoor.
  • Keep RH roughly 30–60 %. Winter ventilation dries the building.
  • VOC sensors are relative; use them where people are not the main source.

11.2 AHU components: fans, filters, coils, dampers

An air handling unit (AHU) is a box of sections in series. Outdoor air enters, is filtered, tempered by heat recovery, heated or cooled, sometimes humidified, and pushed into the supply duct. A second airstream takes air back from the rooms, passes the heat recovery, and leaves as exhaust. Click a component.

The basic sequence

StepActionWhy
1Start heating pump, pre-open heating valve if cold outsideWarm the coil before cold air arrives
2Open outdoor and exhaust dampers, wait for end switchesFans must not run against closed dampers
3Start extract fan, then supply fan, ramp upAvoid over-pressurising the building
4Release the supply temperature loopSequence: heat recovery → heating coil, or heat recovery → cooling
5Monitor: fan feedback, filter Δp, frost, smokeAlarm or trip on fault

The supply temperature loop is a split-range sequence: one controller output from 0 to 100 % is split across actuators. For example 0–33 % cooling valve (inverted), 33–66 % heat recovery, 66–100 % heating valve. Chapter 2 covers the control theory. The supply setpoint itself is often reset from extract temperature (cascade) or from outdoor temperature.

A fan that starts before its dampers are open can collapse ducts or blow flexible connections off. Interlock it in the controller and use end switches, not timers alone.

Where is the frost thermostat of a water heating coil normally placed?

  • Supply side: damper → filter → heat recovery → heat → cool → humidify → fan.
  • Extract side: damper → filter → heat recovery → fan.
  • Start order matters: valves and pumps, dampers, extract fan, supply fan, then loops.
  • The supply temperature loop is a split-range sequence over recovery and coils.

11.3 Heat recovery: plate, rotary, run-around

Ventilation air in winter is a big heat load. 1 m³/s of air heated by 20 K needs about 1.2 kg/m³ × 1.005 kJ/kgK × 1 m³/s × 20 K ≈ 24 kW. Heat recovery takes most of that back from the warm extract air. It is described by the temperature efficiency (EN 308):

η = (tsup,after − tout) ÷ (textract − tout)

η = 0.8 with −10 °C outside and 22 °C extract gives −10 + 0.8 × 32 = 15.6 °C after the exchanger. The heating coil only has to add 4–5 K instead of 30 K.

Counter-flow plate75–90 %No (unless membrane)Very lowBypass damper
Rotary wheel70–85 %Yes (sorption wheels a lot, condensation wheels some)Some carry-over; needs purge sector and correct pressuresWheel speed (≈ 0–10 rpm)
Run-around coil40–60 % (high-end systems more)NoNone, airstreams fully separatePump speed / 3-way valve
Heat pipe40–60 %NoNoneTilt or bypass

Values vary with flow, balance and manufacturer. EU Ecodesign (regulation 1253/2014) set a minimum of 73 % for run-around systems and 67 % for other types in non-residential units since 2018.

How each type works

Plate exchangers pass the two airstreams through alternating thin channels of aluminium or polymer. Heat conducts through the plate. No moving parts. A bypass damper sends outdoor air around the plate when recovery is not wanted, for example on a mild day when the building needs cooling.

Rotary wheels are a slowly turning matrix (often 0–10 rpm, up to ~20 on some designs). Each segment heats up in the extract stream and gives the heat back in the supply stream. Speed controls the output: at very low speed the wheel barely transfers anything. A purge sector flushes the matrix with outdoor air before it enters the supply side, and fan placement must keep supply pressure above extract pressure, or extract air leaks into the supply (EATR, exhaust air transfer ratio).

Run-around coils put one water–glycol coil in each airstream, connected by a pumped loop. The airstreams can be in different parts of the building, fully separate. That makes them the choice for hospitals, labs and retrofits where the ducts do not meet.

Ecodesign-era AHUs often recover 80 % or more. At 10 000 m³/h, −10 °C outside and 22 °C extract that is about 85 kW back from the exhaust. Over a heating season this usually saves several times the extra fan energy needed to push air through the exchanger (typically 100–200 Pa more).
Do not judge a plate's efficiency from temperatures while the bypass is partly open, or while frost protection is active. Log the bypass position and only compute η when it is fully closed.

A hospital wants heat recovery between an operating theatre extract and a ward supply. Which type?

  • η = (t after − t out) ÷ (t extract − t out). 80 % is normal today.
  • Plate: tight, bypass control. Rotary: high η and moisture, but carry-over. Run-around: separate airstreams.
  • Heat recovered = 1.2 × Q (m³/s) × ΔT kW.

11.4 EC fans and pressure/flow control

A fan raises air pressure. The duct system resists flow, and the resistance grows with the square of flow: Δpsystem = k × Q². The fan runs where its curve crosses the system curve. Change the speed and the fan curve moves; the operating point slides along the system curve.

Fan affinity laws

QuantityLawAt 50 % speed
Flow Q∝ n50 %
Pressure Δp∝ n²25 %
Shaft power P∝ n³12.5 %

These hold for a fixed system curve through the origin. In real VAV systems the duct pressure is held at a setpoint, so pressure does not fall to zero and power falls more like n² to n²·⁵. Motor and drive efficiency also drop at low load. Still, the cube law is why speed control is the single biggest fan saving.

EC fans

An EC (electronically commutated) motor is a permanent-magnet motor with built-in power electronics. Compared with an AC induction motor on a belt: no belt losses (3–8 %), higher part-load efficiency, speed control built in. Products like ebm-papst, Ziehl-Abegg or Rosenberg EC fans accept a 0–10 V setpoint and many have a Modbus RTU interface reporting speed, power, temperature and alarms. Large AHUs use a fan array: several small EC fans in parallel. One can fail and the others take over.

What do we control?

ModeMeasuredTypical use

Airflow from an inlet cone: Q = k × √Δp, where the k-factor is printed on the fan. A cone with k = 240 and 400 Pa gives 240 × 20 = 4 800 m³/h. Many EC fans compute this internally and report m³/h directly.

Do not put a fan pressure loop and a VAV box loop on the same fast tuning. If both react within seconds they fight and the whole system hunts. Make the fan loop clearly slower than the box loops, or the boxes clearly slower than the fan.
Specific fan power (SFP) is a common design metric. Many guides and national rules aim for about 1.5–2.0 kW/(m³/s) for a supply plus extract AHU with heat recovery. EN 16798-3 defines SFP classes from SFP1 to SFP7.

A fan on a fixed system curve slows from 100 % to 70 % speed. Roughly what power is left?

  • Q ∝ n, Δp ∝ n², P ∝ n³ on a fixed system curve.
  • EC fans: direct drive, efficient at part load, 0–10 V or Modbus.
  • Control by flow (CAV), duct pressure (VAV) or pressure reset.
  • Q = k√Δp across the inlet cone.

11.5 Demand-controlled ventilation and VAV

Demand-controlled ventilation (DCV) means the airflow follows the actual need: CO₂, VOC, presence or a schedule. It can be done at two levels:

  • Unit level. One AHU serving one big space (a hall, a gym). The extract CO₂ sets the fan speed. Simple and effective.
  • Zone level, with VAV boxes. One AHU serves many rooms. Each room has a variable air volume box: a damper, a flow sensor and a small controller. The room's CO₂ or temperature sets the box's flow setpoint, between Qmin and Qmax.

How a VAV box works

A VAV box is a cascade. The outer loop compares room CO₂ (or temperature) with its setpoint and outputs a flow setpoint. The inner loop measures flow with a cross or orifice sensor (Δp → m³/h) and moves the damper. This is pressure independent: if the duct pressure changes, the inner loop corrects the damper and the room gets the same flow. Belimo, Siemens, Trox and others make compact controllers with the actuator, Δp sensor and Modbus/BACnet/MP-Bus in one housing.

Static pressure reset (trim and respond)

The AHU fan holds duct pressure at a setpoint. If that setpoint is fixed and high, most dampers are throttled: energy is burned as pressure drop across half-closed blades. A better strategy watches the boxes. If no box is near fully open, lower the pressure setpoint a little (trim). If boxes report they cannot reach their flow, raise it (respond). ASHRAE Guideline 36 standardises this.

Control inputBest forWatch out
CO₂Meeting rooms, classrooms, hallsSlow (minutes); does not see building emissions

VOCToilets, kitchens, changing roomsRelative values; can react to perfume or cleaning

If the main AHU fan is supply-flow controlled while the boxes change their flows, the extract side must follow. Otherwise rooms with closed supply boxes go strongly negative and doors whistle. Pair each supply VAV box with an extract box, or control the extract fan to a building pressure.
An office floor with 30 VAV-controlled meeting rooms typically averages 30–50 % of design airflow over the day. With pressure reset, fan energy often falls to 15–30 % of a constant-volume design. The exact figure depends heavily on occupancy patterns.

What does "pressure independent" mean for a VAV box?

  • DCV: airflow follows CO₂, VOC, presence or schedule.
  • VAV box: room loop → flow setpoint → flow loop → damper.
  • Static pressure reset keeps the most-open damper nearly open and saves fan energy.
  • Extract must follow supply, zone by zone or by building pressure.

11.6 Frost protection and defrost

Two different things freeze in an AHU, and they need different protection:

  1. The water heating coil. If cold air passes a coil with stagnant water, the water freezes, expands and splits the tubes. This is a damage event, often a flood.
  2. The heat recovery exhaust side. Warm, humid extract air cools down in the exchanger. Below its dew point, water condenses. Below 0 °C on the surface, it freezes. Ice blocks the channels, airflow drops and recovery fails.

Heating coil frost protection

The frost thermostat is often a capillary (several metres of sensing tube) laid across the whole coil face just after the coil. It trips if any 30–50 cm section drops below its set point (typically 5 °C). Hardware frost thermostats are usually wired directly into the fan and damper circuits so they work even if the controller fails. Many systems also keep a minimum valve opening and run the heating pump whenever it is below about 3–5 °C outside, even with the AHU off.

Heat recovery frost

For balanced flow, the exhaust leaves the exchanger at about tEHA = tETA − η × (tETA − tODA). With 22 °C extract, η = 0.85 and −15 °C outside that is 22 − 0.85 × 37 ≈ −9 °C: frost is certain if the extract air is humid. The drier the extract air, the colder it can go before frost starts. Counter-flow plates freeze earliest, because they are the most efficient. Rotary wheels resist frost better, and sorption wheels best.

StrategyHowCost
Bypass / face-and-bypassSend part of the outdoor air around the plateLess recovery, more coil heat
Section defrostClose one section at a time to melt it with extract airSmall loss, more dampers
Electric or hydronic preheaterWarm outdoor air to about −5 … −3 °C before the plateEnergy, but keeps full flow
Reduce supply flowLess cold air, so the exhaust side stays warmerBuilding goes negative; not allowed everywhere
Rotary: lower wheel speedLess heat transfer, exhaust stays warmerLess recovery
Run-around: glycol temperature limitValve keeps the extract coil inlet above ~−1 °CLess recovery

Frost is detected by an exhaust temperature sensor after the exchanger, or better by the pressure drop across the extract side of the exchanger: ice raises Δp quickly.

A frost alarm that stops the whole AHU is not protection, it is a failure. On a cold night the building then has no ventilation and the heating coil is exposed. Defrost strategies should keep the unit running at reduced recovery; a full stop is the last step.

The heating coil frost thermostat trips. What should the AHU do first?

  • Two frost risks: the water coil (damage) and the exhaust side of heat recovery (ice, blocked flow).
  • Coil frost: hardwired thermostat, fans off, damper shut, valve open, pump on.
  • Recovery frost: bypass, preheat, reduce flow or slow the wheel, detected by temperature or Δp.

11.7 Psychrometrics basics

Psychrometrics is the physics of moist air. Every air handling process can be drawn as a line on a psychrometric chart (or its European cousin, the Mollier h–x diagram, which is the same data rotated). Only a few quantities matter:

QuantitySymbol, unitMeaning
Dry-bulb temperaturet, °CWhat a normal thermometer reads
Absolute humidity (humidity ratio)x, g water / kg dry airHow much water is actually there. Unchanged by heating or cooling without condensation
Relative humidityφ, %Vapour pressure ÷ saturation pressure at that temperature
Dew pointtd, °CTemperature at which this air would reach 100 % RH
Enthalpyh, kJ/kgTotal heat content: sensible + latent (h ≈ 1.006 t + x(2501 + 1.86 t)/1000)

The key fact: warm air can hold much more water. Saturation is about 3.8 g/kg at 0 °C, 7.6 g/kg at 10 °C, 14.7 g/kg at 20 °C and 27 g/kg at 30 °C (at sea level). So heating air lowers RH without changing x, and cooling raises RH until it hits 100 %, then water condenses out.

Processes in an AHU

  • Heating: horizontal line to the right. x constant, RH falls.
  • Heat recovery (plate): also horizontal, unless the exhaust side condenses, which only affects the exhaust stream.
  • Cooling above dew point: horizontal to the left. Cooling below dew point: the air follows down towards the coil's apparatus dew point and loses water.
  • Steam humidification: almost vertical up. Adiabatic: along a line of constant enthalpy (≈ wet-bulb), so the air cools as water evaporates.
  • Mixing two airstreams: the result lies on the straight line between them, weighted by mass flow.

The energy of a process is P = ṁ × Δh. Cooling 1 kg/s of 28 °C, 60 % air (h ≈ 64 kJ/kg) to 13 °C saturated (h ≈ 37 kJ/kg) takes ~27 kW. Only ~15 kW of that is the temperature drop; the rest is latent, condensing water. This is why dehumidification is expensive.

Condensation on cold surfaces is a psychrometric question. A chilled ceiling at 16 °C in a room at 26 °C and 60 % RH (dew point ≈ 17.6 °C) will drip. Chilled-beam controllers therefore watch a dew-point or condensation sensor and raise the water temperature or close the valve.
Never control humidity by RH alone in a space where temperature swings. RH can rise just because the room cooled down. Compute dew point or absolute humidity in the controller when you need a stable moisture signal.

Outdoor air at 0 °C and 90 % RH is heated to 21 °C. What happens?

  • Absolute humidity x is the real amount of water; RH depends on temperature.
  • Heating: horizontal right. Cooling below dew point: down-left, water out.
  • Energy = mass flow × enthalpy difference; latent load is often half of cooling.

11.8 Filters and maintenance

Filters are the part of an AHU that changes the most over time. A clean filter has a low pressure drop. As it collects dust, Δp rises, slowly at first and then faster as the media clogs. The fan must work harder to keep the same airflow, or the airflow falls if the fan is not flow-controlled.

When to change

The common rule: change at the manufacturer's recommended final pressure drop, or at a fixed interval (e.g. 12 months for the supply filter, VDI 6022 has hygiene-based intervals), whichever comes first. Changing too early wastes filters. Changing too late wastes fan energy, and very high Δp can damage the media. The best point balances filter cost against fan energy.

Filter energy: P = Q × Δp ÷ η. At 10 000 m³/h (2.78 m³/s), 150 Pa average and η = 0.6 that is ~0.7 kW, ~2 800 kWh over 4 000 hours. Across a building with ten AHUs this is real money.

A Δp that suddenly drops is not good news. It usually means a filter bag tore, a frame was left open after service, or the sensor hose came off. Alarm on low Δp too.
Wheel drive belts on rotary exchangers are a classic silent failure: the motor runs, the BMS shows "rotor on", but the wheel stands still. A rotation sensor (a magnet and a reed switch on the wheel rim) catches it. Without one, only a sudden drop in recovery efficiency gives it away.

The supply filter Δp falls from 180 Pa to 40 Pa overnight. Most likely cause?

  • ISO 16890 classes by ePM1, ePM2.5, ePM10 and Coarse.
  • Change at final Δp or maximum interval, whichever comes first.
  • Filter energy = Q × Δp ÷ η. Too late is expensive.
  • Alarm on high and low Δp; watch SFP and recovery efficiency for hidden faults.
