Building Automation Course
Chapter 01

Building automation fundamentals

What a building management system is, how it is layered, what it actually reads and writes, and which standards define a "good" one. Everything in later chapters hangs on this vocabulary.

1.1 What a BMS does: monitor, control, optimise

A building management system (BMS) is the set of controllers, networks and software that watches and drives a building's technical systems: heating, ventilation, cooling, lighting, shading, power and often security and AV. You will meet several names for the same idea:

TermMeaning
BMSBuilding Management System. The everyday industry term, often meaning the supervisory software.
BASBuilding Automation System. Common in North America, emphasises the control layer.
BACSBuilding Automation and Control System. The term used by ISO/EN standards.
BEMSBuilding Energy Management System. A BMS whose main focus is metering and energy.

Three jobs

  1. Monitor. Collect values and states: temperatures, pump running, filter dirty, alarm active. Store history. Show it to people.
  2. Control. Act on the plant: open a valve, start a fan, switch a lighting scene. Either automatically (loops, schedules, sequences) or by an operator command.
  3. Optimise. Do the same job with less energy, wear and attention: setbacks when the building is empty, weather compensation, demand-controlled ventilation, alarm routing so problems are fixed early.

What a BMS is not

A BMS coordinates systems. It is not the safety layer. Boiler flame supervision, gas shut-off, fire detection, smoke control and emergency lighting have their own certified controllers. They keep working if the BMS crashes, and a BMS may monitor them but should never be the thing that keeps people safe.

Rule of thumb: if losing the BMS server for a day would make the building unsafe rather than merely less comfortable, the design is wrong.

Try it: what keeps running?

In a typical venue the boilers, air handling units, DALI lighting, audio DSP, intrusion panel and UPS each run on their own controller. A supervisory platform on top shows their state, lets staff send commands within their role and sends alerts. If the supervisory server stops, the boilers still heat and the fire system still protects.

Which of these SHOULD NOT depend on the BMS server being online?

  • BMS, BAS and BACS mean the same thing. Standards say BACS.
  • Monitor, control, optimise.
  • Life-safety functions live in their own certified systems, never in the BMS alone.

1.2 The automation pyramid: field, automation, management

Classic building automation is described in three levels. Each level has its own devices, its own networks and its own time scale. Click a level.

Why the layers matter

Each level should keep working if the one above it fails. A controller with no BMS server must still run its loops with the last setpoints. A field device with no controller must still go to a safe state (a valve spring-returns, a fan stops on frost alarm).

Follow one value through the layers

The pyramid is flattening

Modern devices often speak IP directly: an AHU with a Modbus TCP port, a DALI gateway publishing to MQTT, a smart thermostat with a cloud API. The boundaries blur and the gateway (Chapter 07) becomes a key piece. The layering still applies as a way of thinking: where does this logic run, and what happens when the layer above disappears?

An AHU's frost-protection logic (close outdoor damper, open heating valve, stop the fan) should run at which level?

  • Field = sense and act. Automation = control logic. Management = people and data.
  • Each layer must survive the loss of the one above.
  • Protection and fast loops belong low; overview and optimisation belong high.

1.3 Data points: AI, AO, DI, DO, setpoints, states

Everything a BMS knows is a data point. A medium building has thousands. Physical points come in four basic types:

TypeDirectionValueTypical signalsExamples
AI Analog inputfield → controllernumberPT1000, NTC, 0–10 V, 4–20 mAtemperature, CO₂, pressure
AO Analog outputcontroller → fieldnumber0–10 V, 2–10 V, 4–20 mAvalve position, fan speed
DI Digital inputfield → controlleron/offdry contact, 24 Vpump running, filter alarm, PIR
DO Digital outputcontroller → fieldon/offrelay, triacpump start, boiler enable

Virtual (software) points

Not every point is wired. Controllers and BMS software add points that exist only in logic:

  • Setpoints: the target, e.g. room 21 °C, supply air 18 °C.
  • Modes / multistate: Off / Comfort / Eco / Frost protection.
  • Alarms: derived states such as "temperature 5 K below setpoint for 30 min".
  • Calculated values: averages, run hours, energy totals.

BACnet names these analog-value, binary-value and multi-state-value. In Home Assistant they map to entities like sensor, binary_sensor, number, select and switch.

Command vs. feedback

A good design pairs every important command with feedback: DO "start pump" plus DI "pump running". If they disagree for longer than a delay, raise a discrepancy alarm. Without feedback the BMS only knows what it asked for, not what happened.

Point metadata

A raw number is useless without context. Every point should carry: name, unit, range, scaling (e.g. 4 mA = 0 ppm, 20 mA = 2000 ppm), location, the equipment it belongs to, and how it is updated (polling interval or change-of-value).

Try it: scaling a 4–20 mA signal

A CO₂ sensor sends 4 mA at 0 ppm and 20 mA at 2000 ppm. Why start at 4 and not 0? Because then 0 mA means a broken wire, not "no CO₂". This "live zero" lets the controller tell a fault from a real reading. NAMUR NE43 defines the limits: below 3.6 mA or above 21 mA is a fault.

1.4 Open vs. proprietary systems

"Open" is one of the most abused words in the industry. There are really three questions:

  1. Open protocol? Is the communication standard published and usable by anyone? (BACnet, Modbus, KNX, DALI, MQTT, M-Bus: yes.)
  2. Open engineering? Can any integrator program or reconfigure the controller, or only the vendor's certified partner with a licensed tool?
  3. Open data? Can you get all points out, with history, without paying per point or going through the vendor's cloud?

A system can pass the first test and fail the other two: a controller speaks BACnet, but its program can only be changed with a dongle-protected tool owned by one company.

Open / multi-vendorProprietary / single-vendor
Initial costOften lower, more competitionPackage deals can be cheap up front
IntegrationMix best-of-breed devicesSmooth inside the family, hard outside
ResponsibilitySpread across vendors, integrator owns the gapsOne throat to choke
Lifetime costAny integrator can service itVendor lock-in for every change
Skills neededBroader in-house knowledgeLess, but you depend on the vendor

Where common technologies sit

Cloud dependency

A newer kind of lock-in: devices that are controlled only through a vendor cloud. They are cheap and easy, but the building now depends on an internet link, a foreign server and an API that can change or disappear. Prefer devices with a local API (Modbus, MQTT, local REST) for anything that matters.

A pragmatic mix is common: plant controllers stay vendor-specific (boiler, AHU, DSP) but are reached over open interfaces such as Modbus, MQTT or a documented TCP protocol through small gateways, while the supervisory platform is open source. Consumer devices that only offer a cloud API are the weakest links.

A controller "supports BACnet/IP" but can only be reprogrammed with a vendor tool sold to certified partners. It is…

  • Ask three questions: open protocol, open engineering, open data.
  • Open systems shift responsibility to the integrator.
  • Prefer local APIs over cloud APIs for anything important.

1.5 Standards: EN ISO 16484, EN 15232 / ISO 52120

EN ISO 16484: how BACS are specified and built

The main family of standards for building automation. The parts you will meet most:

  • Part 1: project specification and implementation.
  • Part 2: hardware.
  • Part 3: functions (what a point list and a control function must describe).
  • Part 5: the BACnet data communication protocol.
  • Part 6: BACnet conformance testing.

EN ISO 52120-1 (formerly EN 15232): how much a BACS saves

This standard rates the energy performance of a building's automation in four BACS efficiency classes. EN ISO 52120-1:2022 replaced EN 15232-1; many documents still cite the old number. Each class is defined by which control functions are present: per-room control, demand-based ventilation, pump control, scheduling, monitoring and so on. Drag the slider.

The factors are typical values for thermal energy in offices. Other building types and electrical energy use different tables. The point is the shape: going from D to C saves the most, and every class above C needs more functions and more maintenance to keep the savings.

Why it matters legally (EU)

The Energy Performance of Buildings Directive (EPBD) makes BACS mandatory for larger non-residential buildings. The 2018 revision set a threshold of 290 kW of heating, air-conditioning or combined ventilation output by 2025. The 2024 recast lowers it to 70 kW by the end of 2029. Required capabilities: continuous monitoring and logging of energy use, benchmarking, detecting efficiency losses and talking to other systems. National law transposes the details, so check the local regulation.

Other names you will see

  • VDI 3814 (Germany): practical BACS planning guideline, widely used in Europe.
  • ISO 16739 (IFC) / BIM: building models that BMS data can be linked to.
  • ISA-18.2, ISA-101: alarm management and HMI design (Chapters 20 and 21).
  • IEC 62443: security for automation systems (Chapter 23).

A tender asks for "BACS class B per EN 15232". What do you actually design against today?

  • ISO 16484 = how to specify and build a BACS. Part 5 is BACnet.
  • ISO 52120-1 (ex EN 15232) = efficiency classes A–D. C is the reference.
  • The EPBD makes BACS mandatory above 290 kW now and above 70 kW by the end of 2029.

1.6 Smart building trends: IoT, cloud, digital twins

The core of building automation changes slowly: a valve is still a valve. The layers around it are changing fast.

  1. 1950sPneumaticsAir-pressure signals, local thermostats, no central view.
  2. 1970sCentral supervisionAfter the oil crises: minicomputers start and stop plant to save energy.
  3. 1980sDDCMicroprocessor controllers replace pneumatics. Vendor buses everywhere.
  4. 1990sOpen protocolsBACnet (1995), LON, Modbus, EIB/KNX. Multi-vendor becomes possible.
  5. 2010sIP and IoTEthernet to the field, MQTT, wireless sensors, cloud dashboards.
  6. 2020sData and AISemantic models, FDD, digital twins, open-source platforms.
IT/OT convergence

Building systems now run on the same Ethernet, VLANs, virtual machines and containers as office IT. Benefits: cheaper infrastructure, standard tools, remote access. Costs: OT people need IT skills, and IT security problems now reach the plant room (Chapters 03, 22, 23).

IoT and wireless sensors

Battery sensors (Zigbee, LoRaWAN, Thread) make retrofits cheap: no cables to every room. Trade-offs: battery changes, radio coverage and the fact that a sensor with no reading is not the same as a sensor reading nothing.

Event-driven architectures and MQTT

Instead of a central server polling every device, devices and gateways publish changes to a broker and any number of consumers subscribe. This decouples systems and scales well. It is the reason MQTT has its own chapter (Chapter 06).

Edge and cloud

Control stays on site (the edge). Analytics, long-term storage and fleet dashboards move to the cloud. The rule from 1.1 still holds: the building must run without the cloud.

Semantic models: Haystack, Brick

Tagging points with meaning ("this is the supply air temperature of AHU-1, which serves zone 2") lets software understand a building without a human reading point names. It is the basis for portable analytics.

Digital twins

A digital model of the building linked to live data: geometry from BIM, systems from the BMS. Useful for visualisation, simulation and maintenance planning. In practice, a good point list and accurate as-built drawings already give much of the value.

Analytics, FDD and AI

Fault detection and diagnostics (FDD) runs rules or models over trend data to find problems such as a valve that is stuck, simultaneous heating and cooling or a schedule that no longer matches use. Machine learning adds prediction (optimal start, load forecasting). All of it depends on clean, well-described data (1.3).

Open-source and custom front ends

Platforms such as Home Assistant, Node-RED and Grafana, plus custom web panels, are moving into commercial buildings. They are flexible and free of licence fees. The owner then takes on the support, updates, security and documentation a vendor would normally provide (Chapters 19 and 20).

A modern mid-size building often combines all of this: VLAN-segmented IP networks, an MQTT broker as the integration hub, small custom gateways, a virtualised BMS server, a web panel with role-based access and AV over IP on the same infrastructure.

What must every FDD, AI or digital-twin project have before it can work?

  • The plant changes slowly; networks, software and data change fast.
  • Publish/subscribe (MQTT) and IP everywhere are reshaping integration.
  • Every smart feature depends on good point data.
