Chapter 24

Energy management

Where a building's energy goes, how to measure it honestly, which control strategies save the most, how to report it, and how software finds the faults that quietly waste it. A BMS that does not save energy is only a very expensive set of thermostats.

24.1 Where energy goes in a building

Before you optimise anything, you need a picture of the end uses: what the energy is actually used for. Meters measure carriers (electricity, gas, district heat, oil). Energy management works on end uses (space heating, cooling, fans, lighting, hot water, plug loads). The two rarely line up one to one. A gas meter may feed both space heating and hot water. One electricity meter feeds lighting, fans, chillers, lifts and every laptop charger in the building.

The split depends strongly on building type and climate. In a heating-dominated European climate, space heating is often the largest single end use in offices and schools. In hot climates or in deep-plan offices with high internal gains, cooling and fans take over. Hospitals and hotels have large hot water and 24-hour loads. Data rooms can dominate everything.

Delivered, final and primary energy

Three numbers describe the "same" energy and are easy to confuse:

TermMeaningWhere it is used
Delivered / site energyWhat crosses the building boundary and the meters read.Bills, operational dashboards, most KPIs.
Useful energyHeat or light actually delivered to the space, after plant losses.Engineering calculations.
Primary / source energyDelivered energy times a primary energy factor for the carrier, including generation and grid losses.Energy certificates (EPCs), regulation.

Primary energy factors are set nationally and change as grids decarbonise. Electricity has historically been given factors well above 1 (often 1.8–2.5 in EU methods), natural gas close to 1.1. That is why a kWh of electricity saved can "count" twice as much on a certificate as a kWh of gas, and why a heat pump with a COP of 3 still usually wins on primary energy.

Base load vs weather-dependent load

Split every carrier in two. Base load runs regardless of weather: hot water, lighting, servers, lifts, ventilation fans on a fixed schedule. Weather-dependent load scales with outdoor temperature: space heating and cooling. A night-time and weekend look at the electricity profile shows the base load directly. In many offices the out-of-hours base load is 30–50 % of the daytime peak, which is a strong hint that something runs that should not.

A 6,000 m² office uses about 900 MWh a year: roughly 520 MWh of gas and 380 MWh of electricity. Sub-metering shows that the ventilation fans run 24/7 because a schedule was overridden during commissioning and never reset. Fans of 22 kW total running 128 extra hours a week waste about 145 MWh a year, close to 40 % of the electricity bill. No new hardware is needed to fix it.
Shares from benchmarks are a starting point, not a measurement. Never plan a project around "lighting is 20 %" without sub-meter data or at least a load inventory for the actual building.

An office's electricity at 03:00 on a Sunday is 40 % of its weekday peak. What is the best first step?

  • Meters measure carriers; energy management works on end uses.
  • Heating, cooling, fans and lighting are usually the big controllable end uses.
  • Delivered, useful and primary energy are different numbers. Say which one you mean.
  • The out-of-hours base load is the cheapest place to start.

24.2 Measuring and baselining

"We saved 15 % this year" means nothing unless you know what consumption would have been without the change. That counterfactual is the baseline. It is a model, built from a reference period, that predicts consumption from the things that drive it: weather, occupancy, opening hours, production.

Metering hierarchy

A useful metering plan has three levels. The main meters are the utility's billing meters. Sub-meters split the building by carrier and by major end use: boiler gas, chiller electricity, AHU fans, lighting boards, tenant areas. Check meters or virtual meters close the balance: the sum of sub-meters should match the main meter within a few per cent. An unexplained remainder of more than about 10 % means a meter is wrong or a load is missing.

Degree days

Heating demand grows roughly linearly with how cold it is outside, below a base temperature at which internal gains cover the losses. Heating degree days (HDD) sum that shortfall over a period:

HDD (per day) = max(0, T_base − T_mean_outdoor)
example: T_base 15.5 °C, daily mean 3.5 °C  →  12 HDD

Common base temperatures are 15.5 °C (UK practice), 18 °C or 65 °F (US practice) and the Eurostat method, which counts days below 15 °C against 18 °C. The "right" base differs per building: a well-insulated office with high gains may need no heating above 12 °C. A regression of consumption against HDD at several bases finds the one with the best fit. Cooling degree days (CDD) work the same way above a cooling base.

The regression baseline

Plot monthly (or weekly, or daily) consumption against degree days for a reference year. Fit a line:

E = a + b · HDD
a = base load per period (kWh)
b = weather sensitivity (kWh per degree day)

In the reporting period, feed the actual degree days into the model. The result is the adjusted baseline: what the building would have used this year, with this weather, without the change. Savings are the adjusted baseline minus actual consumption. This is the core of IPMVP Option C (whole-building metering) and of ISO 50015 / ISO 50006 practice.

Try it: raw vs weather-normalised savings

How good is the model?

A baseline is only useful if it predicts well. ASHRAE Guideline 14 gives the commonly quoted limits for a calibrated model: CV(RMSE) within 15 % and NMBE within ±5 % for monthly data, or 30 % and ±10 % for hourly data. R² above about 0.75 is a sensible sanity check. If the fit is poor, look for missing drivers (occupancy, school holidays, a change in opening hours) before trusting any savings figure.

Equally important are non-routine adjustments: a new tenant floor, a server room added, a wing closed for refurbishment. These change consumption for reasons unrelated to your measure and MUST be adjusted for explicitly and documented.

A school installs room-by-room heating control in summer. The following winter is 18 % milder than the reference year. Gas use falls from 1,050 MWh to 790 MWh, a raw drop of 25 %. With the degree-day model the adjusted baseline is 885 MWh, so the measure saved about 95 MWh, or 11 %. The rest was the weather. Reporting 25 % would set the project up for a "savings disappeared" crisis the next cold winter.
Do not use a single change-point line for a building that both heats and cools. Use a model with both HDD and CDD terms (a "5-parameter" change-point model), or split the analysis per carrier.

The baseline is E = 9,000 kWh + 60 kWh/HDD per month. This January had 400 HDD and used 30,000 kWh. What was saved?

  • Savings are always relative to a modelled baseline, never just "last year".
  • E = a + b·HDD splits base load from weather sensitivity.
  • Check fit quality (CV(RMSE), NMBE) and document non-routine adjustments.
  • Sub-meters must reconcile with the main meter.

24.3 Setbacks and optimal start

A building loses heat in proportion to the difference between inside and outside temperature. Lowering the inside temperature when nobody is there reduces that difference, so the losses fall. That is a setback (heating) or setup (cooling). The idea is old and still one of the most effective measures a BMS can implement, because it costs only configuration.

Why setbacks save energy

A common myth says "the building has to reheat in the morning, so you save nothing". It is false for a simple reason: the heat lost overnight depends on the indoor temperature overnight. A cooler building loses less heat. The morning reheat only replaces what was lost, and what was lost was less. The saving is roughly proportional to the temperature reduction times the hours it lasts, adjusted for how quickly the building actually cools down.

That last point matters. A heavy, well-insulated building has a long time constant τ = C / UA (thermal capacity divided by heat loss coefficient), often 20–100 hours. It barely cools in one night, so the setback temperature is never reached and the saving is small. A light, leaky building has a τ of a few hours: it cools fast, saves more per night, but also needs more power to recover.

Optimal start

With a fixed schedule, the heating starts at the same time every day, say 05:00. On a mild day the building is warm by 06:00 and wastes two hours at comfort temperature before anyone arrives. On a very cold day 05:00 may be too late. Optimal start calculates the latest start time that still reaches the comfort setpoint at occupancy. Inputs are the current indoor temperature, the outdoor temperature and a learned heating rate (K per hour) for this building. Most controllers adapt the rate after each morning by comparing predicted and actual warm-up times. Optimal stop does the reverse: switch off before the end of occupancy if the building will coast within a comfort band until people leave.

Try it: one winter day

Rules of thumb you will see quoted, for example from the US Department of Energy for homes, are on the order of up to 10 % of annual heating energy for a 4–5 K setback over 8 hours a day. Offices and schools with long nights and weekends often do better, because they are unoccupied for about 70 % of the hours in a year.

Limits and side effects

  • Frost and condensation. Keep a frost protection floor (often 5–8 °C for spaces, higher where water pipes run near external walls) and watch humidity in buildings with poor insulation.
  • Slow systems. Underfloor heating in a thick screed responds in many hours. Setbacks of 1–2 K are typical; deep setbacks cause long recovery and uncomfortable mornings.
  • Heat pumps. Fast recovery at high flow temperature lowers the COP, and electric backup heaters may switch on. A shallow setback with a longer, gentler recovery is usually better.
  • Peak demand. Every zone recovering at 06:00 creates a morning peak. Staggering starts per zone helps with demand charges.
A 1970s school with lightweight construction (τ ≈ 8 h) runs heating 06:00–16:00 on weekdays with a 14 °C setback. Before, a fixed start at 04:00 was used all winter. Optimal start moved the average start to 05:20 and cut about 1.5 boiler hours on mild days, saving roughly 6 % of heating gas on top of the setback itself.
Weekend and holiday calendars are where setbacks quietly fail. A Monday-morning recovery after a 60-hour weekend needs a longer lead time, and a holiday that is not in the BMS calendar means a whole empty building heated to comfort. Check the calendar every year.

Two identical offices use a 5 K night setback. One is heavyweight concrete (τ = 60 h), one is lightweight (τ = 6 h). Which saves more from the setback?

  • Setbacks save because a cooler building loses less heat; the morning reheat does not cancel that.
  • Savings depend on the time constant τ = C/UA.
  • Optimal start finds the latest start that still meets comfort on time.
  • Slow systems and heat pumps want shallow setbacks.

24.4 Event-driven and occupancy-based control

A time schedule assumes the building is used as planned. Real rooms are not. Meeting rooms are booked and then empty. Classrooms stand empty during exams. A hotel room is sold but the guest is out all day. Occupancy-based control conditions a space only when it is used, using events from sensors and systems instead of a clock.

Sources of occupancy information

SourceWhat it tells youStrengthsWeaknesses

Three room states

Good occupancy control is not on/off. EN ISO 52120-1 and most room controllers use three or four modes:

  • Comfort: occupied. Full setpoint, ventilation on demand, lights on.
  • Standby (pre-comfort): scheduled but nobody detected. Setpoint 1–2 K away from comfort, so the room recovers within minutes when someone comes in. Ventilation at minimum.
  • Economy / unoccupied: outside schedule. Full setback, ventilation off.
  • Protection: frost or overheat protection only, for example when a window is open.

The window contact is a classic event: open the window, the room goes to protection mode and the heating valve closes. No energy goes straight out of the window.

Try it: a meeting room's day

Demand-controlled ventilation

Ventilation is sized for full occupancy, but most rooms are at full occupancy for a small fraction of the time. Demand-controlled ventilation (DCV) modulates the air flow on CO₂. A typical sequence holds minimum flow below 600–800 ppm and reaches full flow at 1,000–1,200 ppm. Because fan power grows roughly with the cube of flow, running at 50 % flow needs well under half the power in theory (real systems with fixed pressure setpoints save less). Heating and cooling the outdoor air drops in proportion to the flow.

Event-driven architecture

On the software side, occupancy control is a natural fit for events: a PIR change, a booking starting, a door card swipe. Each event updates a room state machine. Two design rules matter. First, the controller that owns the room keeps the state machine, so it keeps working when the network or the supervisory system is down. Second, every event source has a timeout fallback: if a sensor goes silent, the room falls back to the schedule, not to "unoccupied forever" or "comfort forever".

A hotel links the key-card holder to the room controller. Without a card the room goes to standby after 10 minutes (heating 2 K lower, fan coil on low, lights off). When the booking system marks the room as unsold, it goes to economy with a 6 K setback. Studies and vendor data for such systems commonly report HVAC savings in the range of 20–40 % per room, depending on climate and occupancy rate.
A hold-off timer that is too short switches lights and comfort off while people sit still reading. Users respond by taping over sensors or asking for "always on", and all savings are lost. Start with 15–20 minutes for offices and tune with data.

Why does a room in "standby" mode keep its setpoint only 1–2 K below comfort?

  • Condition spaces when they are used, not when the clock says.
  • Use comfort, standby, economy and protection modes rather than on/off.
  • DCV on CO₂ cuts fan and outdoor-air energy.
  • Keep the state machine local and give every event source a timeout fallback.

24.5 KPIs and reporting (ISO 50001)

Saving energy once is a project. Keeping it saved is a management system. ISO 50001 (current edition 2018) defines an energy management system (EnMS): a set of roles, processes and records that makes an organisation keep improving its energy performance. It does not prescribe technology. It does require data, and a BMS is usually the main source.

The ISO 50001 cycle

Supporting standards fill in the detail: ISO 50006 covers baselines and EnPIs, ISO 50015 covers measurement and verification, and ISO 50002 covers energy audits. In the EU, large enterprises must either run a certified energy management system or have periodic energy audits under the Energy Efficiency Directive. The thresholds changed in the 2023 recast, so check current national law.

Reports people actually read

A good energy report fits on one page per audience. Management wants cost, carbon and trend against target. The facilities team wants exceptions: which meter, which day, which deviation. Engineers want the trend that explains it. Typical cadences are a daily exception list, a monthly EnPI report and an annual review. Use CUSUM charts (cumulative sum of actual minus baseline) to show when savings started, or stopped. A flat CUSUM line means "as baseline"; a falling line means savings are accumulating.

A retail chain with 120 stores ranks every store weekly by weather-normalised kWh/m². The bottom ten get a remote check of schedules and setpoints by the central energy team. Most outliers turn out to be overrides: a store manager who set the heating to "permanent comfort" during a cold snap and forgot it.
KPIs invite gaming. Divide by area and a building that adds a storage hall looks better; divide by occupants and one that crams in staff looks better. Always show absolute consumption next to intensity metrics.

In ISO 50001, what is a significant energy use (SEU)?

  • ISO 50001 is a Plan-Do-Check-Act management system; the BMS feeds its Check step.
  • EnB, EnPI and SEU are the core terms; ISO 50006 and 50015 give the method.
  • EUI in kWh/m²·a is the headline KPI; normalise heating for degree days.
  • Show absolute values beside intensities, and use CUSUM to show trends.

24.6 Fault detection and diagnostics (FDD)

Buildings drift. Sensors go out of calibration, valves stick, someone overrides a schedule and leaves. None of this raises an alarm, because the building still feels fine. It just uses more energy. Studies of commercial buildings, including work by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, commonly find that faults and poor operation waste somewhere between 5 % and 30 % of energy. FDD software finds these problems automatically from trend data.

Detection vs diagnosis

Detection says that something is wrong: "heating and cooling valves are open at the same time". Diagnosis says why: "the cooling valve is leaking" or "the heating valve has a broken actuator". Detection is much easier. Many products stop there and leave diagnosis to an engineer, ideally with the relevant trends attached.

Three families of methods

MethodHow it worksProsCons
Rule-basedExpert rules on point values, e.g. the NIST APAR rules for AHUsTransparent, explainable, works from day oneThresholds need tuning; needs good point metadata
Model-basedCompare measurements with a physical model (e.g. expected coil ΔT)Can detect subtle degradationModel building effort per system
Data-drivenStatistical or machine-learning models of "normal", flagging anomaliesLittle engineering effortHard to explain; needs clean history; learns existing faults as normal

Anatomy of a rule

A useful rule has four parts: a condition, a threshold with a dead band for sensor error, a persistence time so short transients do not trigger it, and a mode filter so it only runs when it makes sense (fan on, not during start-up, not in test).

rule  simultaneous_heating_cooling
when  fan_status = on  and  not start_up(30 min)
if    heating_valve > 5 %  and  cooling_valve > 5 %
for   ≥ 2 h
then  fault "Simultaneous heating and cooling", cost estimate attached

From fault to fix

FDD only saves energy when someone acts. Good systems rank faults by estimated cost (kWh and money per day) and by comfort impact, so the team fixes the expensive ones first. They link faults to work orders and re-check after the fix. Semantic tagging (Brick, Project Haystack) lets the same rule library run on any building without hand-mapping each point.

An FDD platform runs about 40 rules on a 25,000 m² campus with 14 AHUs. In the first month it flags 160 issues. After grouping duplicates and ranking by cost, the top five are: two leaking cooling valves causing simultaneous heating and cooling, one economiser damper stuck at minimum, one supply air sensor reading 3 K high, and a ventilation schedule overridden to 24/7. Fixing those five recovers most of the estimated savings.
Too many alerts kill FDD faster than too few. If the first week produces hundreds of unranked faults, the team stops reading them. Start with a few high-value rules, tune thresholds and persistence, and only then widen the library.

Why does an FDD rule need a persistence time?

  • Faults waste energy silently; FDD finds them from trends.
  • Rules need a condition, threshold, persistence and mode filter.
  • Rank by cost and close the loop with work orders.
  • Fewer, well-tuned rules beat many noisy ones.
