Chapter 19

BMS software platforms

Controllers keep the building running. The supervisory platform is where people see it, change it, get alarmed by it and learn from its history. This chapter compares the big commercial platforms, the SCADA ideas they all share, and Home Assistant as an open-source alternative, down to its entity model, automation engine and data storage. It ends with an honest look at where open source stops being enough.

19.1 Commercial BMS: Desigo, Niagara, EcoStruxure

Three platforms dominate many commercial tenders in Europe and beyond: Siemens Desigo CC, Tridium Niagara and Schneider Electric EcoStruxure Building Operation (EBO). Others matter too: Honeywell, Johnson Controls Metasys, Priva, Sauter, Beckhoff-based systems and many regional vendors. The three are worth studying because they represent three different philosophies.

Niagara in a little more depth

Niagara is the most common one to meet as a software engineer, because it is a framework, not a product. A station is a tree of components. Each component has slots: properties, actions and topics. Logic is built by linking slots on a wire sheet. Drivers (BACnet, Modbus, KNX, LON and many more) appear as components that expose remote points as local ones. Stations talk to each other with the Fox protocol (TCP 1911, or 4911 for the TLS variant foxs). Points carry tags (Niagara's own and Project Haystack) that drive queries, navigation and templates.

Because many OEMs (Honeywell, Distech Controls, Johnson Controls and others) ship Niagara under their own brand, the same skills transfer. The catch: each OEM licence is often "branded", so a JACE bought from one vendor may not open a station licensed for another.

A 25,000 m² office building on Niagara might run eight JACEs, one per plant room and riser, each with 300 to 1,500 points over BACnet MS/TP and Modbus. A Supervisor VM aggregates histories and alarms from all of them, serves HTML5 graphics to operators, and exports trends to an energy platform. The same building on Desigo CC would have PXC controllers per plant, a Desigo CC server with SQL Server, and the fire panels (Cerberus) shown on the same station.
"Open protocol" is not the same as "open platform". A system can speak BACnet perfectly and still lock you in through proprietary graphics, engineering tools, licences tied to a dealer, or controller programs only one integrator can edit. Ask who owns the engineering files and the licence keys at handover.

What best describes Niagara's position compared with Desigo CC?

  • Desigo CC: integrated single-vendor management station. Niagara: OEM framework. EBO: one software model from edge server to enterprise.
  • All three follow the same layers: clients, server, edge controllers, field protocols.
  • Lock-in hides in tools, licences and engineering files, not in the field protocol.

19.2 SCADA concepts

SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition) comes from utilities and industry, but every BMS supervisory layer is a SCADA system in disguise. The core building blocks are the same in Desigo CC, Niagara, Ignition, WinCC or a home-grown front end:

BlockJobConcrete numbers
Driver / I/O serverTalks the field protocol. Polls or subscribes.Modbus poll every 1–10 s; BACnet COV with 0.2 K increment.
Tag (point) databaseOne record per value: address, type, units, scaling, quality, timestamp.5,000–50,000 tags for a large building.
Alarm engineEvaluates limits and states, tracks acknowledge and return to normal.High limit 26 °C, deadband 0.5 K, delay 300 s.
HistorianStores time series, often with deadband or swinging-door compression.1,000 tags × 1 value/min ≈ 525 million values/year.
HMIGraphics, trends, alarm lists, operator commands.Screen update 1–2 s; command round trip under 2 s.
Scheduler and logicTime programs, calendars, supervisory sequences.Weekly schedule, holiday calendar, optimum start.

Every tag value carries three things: value, timestamp and quality (good, uncertain, bad; OPC UA has a full status-code system). Quality is what makes SCADA honest: a dead Modbus gateway turns 400 tags "bad" instead of leaving the last value on screen looking fine.

Try it: follow one value through a SCADA stack

The automation pyramid, again

The ISA-95 / Purdue levels from Chapter 01 map directly: level 0 sensors and actuators, level 1 controllers, level 2 supervisory (SCADA/BMS server and HMI), level 3 site operations (energy management, maintenance), level 4 enterprise IT. A healthy design keeps control at levels 1 and below. Level 2 supervises: it changes setpoints and schedules but never closes a fast loop over the network.

A district heating substation PLC runs its own PID loops in 100 ms cycles. The SCADA server polls it every 5 s, historises supply and return temperatures with a 0.2 K deadband, and raises a "supply below 55 °C for 10 min" alarm. When the server is down for a Windows update, heat still flows; only the graphics and history pause.
Polling cost grows quietly. 20,000 Modbus registers at 1 s across slow serial gateways will not fit: at 9,600 baud a single Modbus RTU read of 10 registers takes roughly 30–40 ms including turnaround. Group registers into block reads and give each tag a scan class that matches how fast it really changes.

A Modbus gateway dies. What should a correct SCADA show for its 400 tags?

  • Driver, tag database, alarms, historian, HMI, logic: every BMS has them.
  • Every value is value + timestamp + quality. Quality prevents stale data from lying.
  • Supervise from level 2; control at level 1.

19.3 Home Assistant as a BMS

Home Assistant (HA) is an open-source home automation platform written in Python, with a release every month and thousands of integrations. It was built for homes, but its core is a general event-driven state engine. That makes it usable as a light supervisory layer for small commercial buildings, as long as you understand what it is and what it is not.

The core has three central objects:

  • Event bus. Everything that happens is an event: state_changed, call_service, automation_triggered, homeassistant_start.
  • State machine. The current state of every entity, kept in memory. Writing a new state fires a state_changed event.
  • Service (action) registry. Named operations such as light.turn_on or climate.set_temperature. In 2024 the user-facing name changed from "services" to "actions"; the concept is the same.

Around the core sit integrations (drivers), the recorder (history database), the automation engine, and the frontend, which talks to the core through a WebSocket API. A REST API also exists. On Home Assistant OS, a Supervisor manages the core container plus add-ons (apps) such as an MQTT broker, Node-RED or a database.

A boutique hotel with 30 rooms uses KNX for lighting and room heating, a Modbus heat pump, an MQTT-based energy meter gateway and a networked audio system. Home Assistant on a small VM integrates all four, runs schedules and occupancy logic, and serves a custom tablet UI. Safety-related functions (fire, heat pump protection, frost protection) stay in their own controllers.

Where does Home Assistant keep the current value of sensor.room_101_temperature?

  • HA core = event bus + state machine + action registry.
  • Integrations are drivers; the recorder is the historian; the frontend is just a client of the API.
  • It fits small supervisory roles well. Control and safety stay in field controllers.

19.4 HA: entities, integrations, templates, helpers

The data model is the part you must understand precisely, because everything else hangs on it.

ObjectWhat it isExample
IntegrationCode for one protocol or product (a domain like modbus, knx, mqtt).modbus
Config entryOne configured instance of an integration (one gateway, one account)."Heat pump gateway 10.0.20.15"
DeviceA physical or logical device in the device registry: manufacturer, model, firmware."Heat pump HP-1"
EntityOne value or control. Has an entity_id (domain.object_id) and a stable unique_id.sensor.hp1_flow_temperature
Area / floorWhere things are. Devices and entities can be assigned to areas; areas to floors."Plant room", floor "Basement"
LabelFree tags across everything, for grouping and targeting."critical", "energy"

A state object has a state string (maximum 255 characters), a dictionary of attributes and three timestamps: last_changed (state string changed), last_updated (state or attributes changed) and last_reported (any write, even identical). States are always strings: "21.4", "on", "unavailable", "unknown". Numbers become numbers only when you convert them.

Try it: entity model explorer

Templates

Templates are Jinja2 expressions evaluated against the state machine. They appear in template entities, conditions, action data and notifications.

{# average of two room sensors, ignoring dead ones #}
{% set vals = ['sensor.r101_temp', 'sensor.r102_temp']
   | map('states') | reject('in', ['unknown','unavailable'])
   | map('float') | list %}
{{ (vals | sum / vals | count) | round(1) if vals else none }}

Useful functions: states('sensor.x') (returns "unknown" rather than failing), is_state('switch.pump', 'on'), state_attr('climate.r101', 'temperature'), float(default), has_value('sensor.x'), now(), area_entities('Plant room'). A template entity re-renders when an entity it references changes.

Helpers

Helpers are entities you create, not ones a device provides. They hold operator settings and derived values.

Comfort setpoint the facility manager can change (input_number)
"Heat pump COP" calculated from heat and electrical power
Outdoor temperature read from a KNX weather station
Weekly occupancy schedule for the conference room
Monthly energy counter that resets on the 1st
"Any window open on floor 2" binary sensor
Every entity can become unavailable or unknown. A template like {{ states('sensor.a') | float + 1 }} fails when the sensor is unavailable, because float without a default raises an error. Always give defaults or test has_value() first, and decide what an automation should do when its input is missing.

A sensor sends the same 21.4 °C twice. Which timestamp moves?

  • Integration → config entry → device → entity; areas, floors and labels organise them.
  • States are strings with attributes and three timestamps. Treat unavailable as normal.
  • Helpers hold operator settings; templates derive values; integrations bring device data.

19.5 HA: automations, scripts, traces

An automation is three lists: triggers (when to start), conditions (whether to continue) and actions (what to do). A script is just an action sequence with a name, callable from anywhere. Both are YAML, edited in the UI or in files.

PartCommon typesNotes
Triggersstate, numeric_state, time, time_pattern, event, mqtt, sun, template, homeassistant (start)A trigger fires on an edge. numeric_state fires when the value crosses the threshold, not while it stays above.
Conditionsstate, numeric_state, time, template, and/or/notEvaluated once, at trigger time.
Actionscall an action, delay, wait_for_trigger, choose, if, repeat, parallel, variablesLong waits make the run "alive" for minutes; that is where modes matter.

Try it: build an automation

Modes: what happens when it triggers again while running

Imagine an automation "door opened → light on → wait 5 min → light off". The door opens again at minute 2. The mode decides:

ModeBehaviourGood for
single (default)New trigger ignored while running; a warning is logged.One-shot reactions.
restartStops the current run, starts again.Motion-style "keep the light on" timers.
queuedRuns one after another (default max 10).Sequenced commands to a slow device.
parallelRuns simultaneously (default max 10).Independent per-trigger work, e.g. per-room notifications.

Traces

Every run stores a trace: which trigger fired, the trigger variables, each condition's result, each action's result and timing, and the context chain. By default the last 5 traces per automation are kept (trace: stored_traces: changes this). Traces are the first place to look when "the automation did not run": usually it did run, and a condition said no.

An office has "CO₂ above 1000 ppm for 2 min → ventilation 80 %". A trace shows that at 10:14 the trigger fired, but the condition "room occupied" was false because the presence sensor was unavailable. The fix is not in the automation logic; it is a dead Zigbee sensor.
A numeric_state trigger fires once when crossing the threshold. If Home Assistant restarts while CO₂ is already at 1,400 ppm, nothing fires. Robust automations add a homeassistant: start trigger and re-check the condition, or use a template binary sensor and act on its state.

A "motion → light on, wait 3 min, light off" automation should keep the light on while motion continues. Which mode?

  • Triggers are edges; conditions are checked once; actions may wait.
  • Choose the mode deliberately: single, restart, queued, parallel.
  • Read the trace before changing the logic.

19.6 Time-series data: recorder, InfluxDB, Grafana

Home Assistant's recorder writes every state change and most events to a SQL database (SQLite file home-assistant_v2.db by default). It keeps 10 days by default (purge_keep_days) and purges nightly. It is built for the History panel and debugging, not for years of trend analysis.

On top, HA computes statistics for entities with a state_class (measurement, total, total_increasing): 5-minute short-term statistics (purged with the states) and hourly long-term statistics (mean, min, max, or sum) kept indefinitely. The Energy dashboard reads these. They are compact: one row per entity per hour.

Data flow

Try it: storage estimator

InfluxDB is a dedicated time-series database. The HA InfluxDB integration writes each state change as a point in line protocol, for example °C,entity_id=r101_temp value=21.4 1728111600000000000 (by default the measurement is the unit). InfluxDB 1.x is queried with InfluxQL, 2.x with Flux or InfluxQL, and 3.x with SQL. Columnar compression makes a slowly varying float cost a few bytes. Grafana sits on top: dashboards, heatmaps, alerting, and it can also read PostgreSQL/TimescaleDB, Prometheus and others. Commercial BMS do the same internally: Desigo CC keeps trends in SQL Server; Niagara keeps histories per station and can export them.

A school with 1,200 entities changes about 15 times per hour each. That is roughly 430,000 rows per day. The recorder with 10 days needs on the order of 1 GB. The same data for five years in InfluxDB is a few gigabytes. The facility team keeps the HA recorder short, excludes noisy entities, and uses Grafana on InfluxDB for multi-year energy reports.
SQLite on an SD card with a high write rate wears the card and corrupts databases on power loss. Exclude chatty entities (signal strength, uptime, per-second power) from the recorder with exclude filters, use an SSD, and back up the database while HA is stopped or via HA's own backup.

You need hourly average room temperature for the last three years in HA's own UI. What provides it without extra databases?

  • Recorder: raw history, short retention, for debugging and the History view.
  • Long-term statistics: hourly aggregates, kept forever, needs state_class.
  • InfluxDB/TimescaleDB + Grafana for years of raw trends and analysis.

19.7 Limits of open-source platforms in commercial buildings

Open-source platforms are technically capable. The limits in commercial buildings are mostly about accountability, certification and operations over 15–25 years, not about features.

RequirementCommercial BMSHome Assistant / open source
Tender compliance (e.g. "BTL-listed B-OWS", EN ISO 16484)Listed products, documented.Generally not certified.
Operator roles, audit trail of commandsFine-grained rights per area and point; audit log standard.Admin / user only; logbook shows changes but is not a tamper-resistant audit trail.
Alarm management (priorities, acknowledge, shelving)Built in, often aligned with ISA-18.2 ideas.Must be built from helpers and automations, or a custom front end.
Redundancy / hot standbyServer redundancy options exist on major platforms.No native HA clustering; rely on VM-level restart and backups.
Support and liabilityContracted SLA, certified integrators, spare parts for 10+ years.Community, or your own team; you carry the liability.
Change paceMajor releases every 1–2 years, long-term support.Monthly releases; deprecations within months.
Cost of entryLicences plus engineering, often tens of thousands of euros.Free software; cost is in engineering and upkeep.
Integration breadthStrong on BACnet/Modbus/OPC, weak on consumer IoT and AV.Very broad, quality uneven.

A common hybrid

Many projects land on a hybrid: certified controllers and a commercial or Niagara supervisor for HVAC and anything in the tender's compliance scope; fire and security on their own certified systems; an open platform or a custom front end on top for user experience, AV, IoT and analytics, reading through BACnet/IP, MQTT or an API. The open layer adds value; it never becomes the only thing that keeps the building safe.

A 12,000 m² office tender asks for a BTL-listed operator workstation, a five-level operator rights model and 24/7 vendor support. A commercial BMS is the safe answer. The tenant then adds Home Assistant behind its own VLAN to integrate meeting-room panels and AV, reading occupancy and temperature from the BMS via BACnet/IP read-only. If HA dies, nobody is cold.
The "bus factor" is real. A building run on one engineer's custom automations becomes unmaintainable when that person leaves. If you use open source commercially, document it as you would a commercial system: point lists, architecture, backup and restore procedure, upgrade policy, and a named support owner.

Which requirement most clearly rules out Home Assistant as the sole BMS?

  • The limits are certification, audited operation, support and long-term stability.
  • Hybrids work: certified core, open layer on top, read-mostly.
  • Document open systems as rigorously as commercial ones.
