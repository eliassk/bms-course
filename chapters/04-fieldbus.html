Chapter 04

Serial and fieldbus protocols

The wires below the IP network. RS-485 and the protocols that ride on it, the dedicated building buses (M-Bus, KNX, DALI, LON), boiler buses, and the humble analog signal. Most building faults that "look like software" are found here.

4.1 RS-485 physical layer: wiring, termination, bias

RS-485 (TIA/EIA-485-A) is not a protocol. It only defines the electrical layer: how a 0 and a 1 look on a pair of wires. Modbus RTU, BACnet MS/TP, many vendor buses and most heat pump and inverter ports run on top of it. Get the physical layer wrong and no amount of protocol knowledge will help.

Differential signalling

RS-485 uses two wires, usually called A and B. The driver pushes them in opposite directions and the receiver looks only at the difference. Noise picked up along the cable hits both wires almost equally (common-mode noise) and cancels out in the subtraction. That is why a twisted pair can carry data for 1200 m through a plant room full of drives and contactors.

A and B are not standardised in practice. In the standard, A is the inverting line and B the non-inverting one, so in the idle (mark, logic 1) state B is more positive than A. Many manufacturers label them the other way, or use D+/D−, TX+/TX−. If a device does not answer at all, swapping the pair is a legitimate first test.

Topology: a line, not a star

At these speeds the cable behaves as a transmission line. A signal edge travels down the cable and, at any point where the impedance changes (an open end, a branch), part of it reflects back. Reflections add to later edges and cause ringing. The cure has two parts:

  1. Daisy chain. Run the cable in to each device and out to the next. Stubs (short branches) should be as short as possible, ideally a few tens of centimetres. Stars and long branches create reflections that no termination can fully remove.
  2. Terminate both ends with 120 Ω across A–B, matching the cable impedance, so the edge is absorbed instead of reflected. Only the two physical ends, never the middle devices.

Biasing (fail-safe)

When no device is transmitting, every driver is off and the bus floats. Two 120 Ω resistors pull A and B together, so the differential voltage sits near 0 V, inside the undefined ±200 mV band. Receivers then read random bits and UARTs see false start bits. Bias resistors fix this: a pull-up on B and a pull-down on A (typically 390–680 Ω, at one point on the bus, often the master) hold the idle bus at a clear logic 1. Many modern transceivers have built-in "true fail-safe" receivers that treat 0 V as a 1.

The third wire and shielding

"Two-wire RS-485" is a myth that causes many site faults. Receivers need the common-mode voltage within −7…+12 V of their own ground. If devices are powered from different supplies or distribution boards, their grounds can differ by several volts. Run a signal common (the third conductor, often labelled GND, COM or 0V) between all devices. Connect the cable shield to protective earth at one point only, so it does not carry earth loop currents.

A typical Modbus trunk in a plant room: 8 devices (heat pump, two inverters, energy meters, a VAV controller) over 150 m of shielded twisted pair, e.g. a Belden 3105A type cable or a LiYCY 2×2×0.5. One pair for A/B, one conductor of the second pair as common. 120 Ω at the gateway and at the last meter. Bias enabled only on the gateway. Stubs into each device under 30 cm.

A Modbus bus works until the master stops polling, then every device logs framing errors. Most likely cause?

  • RS-485 is only the electrical layer: differential, multi-drop, half-duplex.
  • Daisy chain, short stubs, 120 Ω at both physical ends, bias at one point.
  • Always run a signal common. Earth the shield at one end.

4.2 Modbus RTU

Modbus was published by Modicon in 1979 and is now maintained by the Modbus Organization. It is the lowest common denominator of building automation: almost every heat pump, inverter, meter and chiller speaks it. Its strength is simplicity. Its weakness is the same: Modbus moves numbers, and says nothing about what they mean.

Master and slaves

One master (the specification now says client) sends a request. One slave (server) answers. Slaves never speak unprompted. Addresses run 1–247. Address 0 is broadcast: every slave executes a write, nobody answers. There is exactly one master per RS-485 segment.

The data model

The "40001" style is a documentation convention, not what is on the wire. On the wire, register 40001 is offset 0 in the holding register table. Some manuals list offsets, some list 1-based numbers, some mix both. The infamous "off by one" error, reading the register next to the one you wanted, comes from this.

The RTU frame

In RTU mode the bytes are sent as binary, usually at 9600 or 19200 bit/s. The specification's default is 8 data bits, even parity, 1 stop bit (8E1); many devices ship with 8N1. Frames are separated by silence: at least 3.5 character times (about 4 ms at 9600 bit/s) between frames, and no gap longer than 1.5 characters inside a frame. A request has four parts:

  1. Slave address, 1 byte.
  2. Function code, 1 byte.
  3. Data, 0–252 bytes. For a read: start address (2 bytes) and quantity (2 bytes), big-endian.
  4. CRC-16, 2 bytes, polynomial 0xA001 (reflected 0x8005), initial value 0xFFFF, sent low byte first. This is the one little-endian field in Modbus.

Exceptions

If a slave understands the request but cannot serve it, it answers with the function code plus 0x80 and one exception code. No answer at all means something else: wrong address, wrong baud rate or parity, wiring, or a CRC error the slave silently discarded.

CodeNameTypical cause
01Illegal functionDevice does not support e.g. FC 16, only FC 06
02Illegal data addressRegister does not exist (often the off-by-one error)
03Illegal data valueQuantity too large (FC 03 allows at most 125 registers) or value out of range
04Server device failureInternal error in the slave
06Server device busyTry again later

Data types: everything is a 16-bit register

Modbus only knows 16-bit words. Everything else is convention, documented (hopefully) in the vendor's register map:

  • Scaled integers: 225 with a factor of 0.1 means 22.5 °C. Signed values use two's complement: 0xFFF6 is −10, i.e. −1.0 °C.
  • 32-bit values (energy counters, IEEE 754 floats) span two registers. The word order is not defined: some devices send the high word first, others the low word ("word swap"). Read a known value and check.
  • Bit fields: one register holds 16 status flags.

Polling and timing

Each request-response takes time on the wire. At 9600 8E1, one character is 11 bits, about 1.15 ms. Reading 10 registers needs 8 bytes out and 25 bytes back, roughly 38 ms plus turnaround and the 3.5-character gaps. Add the slave's response delay (often 20–100 ms) and a single poll costs around 100 ms. A bus with 20 devices and 5 requests each takes about 10 s per cycle. Group adjacent registers into one request and poll slow values slowly.

A manual says "holding register 40201". Your client library asks for a zero-based address. What do you enter?

  • One master, up to 247 slaves, request-response only.
  • Four tables: coils, discrete inputs, input registers, holding registers.
  • Frame = address, function, data, CRC (low byte first). Silence separates frames.
  • Meaning, scaling and word order come only from the vendor's register map.

4.3 M-Bus and wireless M-Bus

M-Bus (Meter-Bus, EN 13757-2 and -3) was designed for one job: reading utility meters (heat, water, gas, electricity) cheaply over long distances. It is common in district heating, apartment blocks and sub-metering in commercial buildings.

A bus that powers the meters

M-Bus is two wires, polarity-free, in any topology except a ring. The master (a "level converter" or M-Bus gateway) supplies the bus with power and talks to the meters in two different ways:

  • Master → meter: voltage modulation. A logic 1 (mark) is the full bus voltage, typically around 36 V. A logic 0 (space) drops it by about 12 V.
  • Meter → master: current modulation. Each meter draws a constant quiescent current (one unit load, ≤ 1.5 mA) for a 1. For a 0 it adds 11–20 mA. The master detects the extra current.

Because the meter never has to drive voltage, its interface is cheap and can even be bus-powered, so battery meters last 10 years or more. Speeds are low, usually 2400 bit/s (300–9600 allowed).

Addressing

  • Primary address: 1 byte. 1–250 for meters, 0 for unconfigured devices, 253 for secondary addressing, 254 and 255 broadcast. Primary addresses must be set by hand and collide easily.
  • Secondary address: 8 bytes, built from the meter's serial number, manufacturer code, version and medium. Unique from the factory. A master can find all meters with a wildcard search, which takes minutes on a large bus.

Meters answer a REQ_UD2 request with a RSP_UD telegram containing data records. Each record carries a DIF/VIF header that encodes the data type, unit and scale (kWh, m³, °C), so unlike Modbus the meaning travels with the value.

Wireless M-Bus

wM-Bus (EN 13757-4) moves the same application layer onto radio. Battery meters wake up, transmit a short telegram every few seconds or minutes, and sleep. A receiver (gateway, drive-by reader or data collector) only listens. Telegrams are usually AES-128 encrypted with a per-meter key supplied by the meter vendor. The Open Metering System (OMS) specification pins down the options so meters from different vendors interoperate.

A 40-apartment block: one heat meter and two water meters per flat, 120 meters on one wired M-Bus master in the basement, read every 15 minutes and forwarded to the BMS over Modbus TCP. In a retrofit of a similar block, wM-Bus meters (mode C1) are read by two receivers in the stairwells instead, saving the cabling but adding a key-management task: every meter's AES key must be loaded into the gateway.

Why can M-Bus meters run for years on a battery, or entirely from the bus?

  • M-Bus: polarity-free, bus-powered, voltage down, current up.
  • Primary addresses 1–250, unique 8-byte secondary addresses.
  • Telegrams are self-describing (DIF/VIF), unlike Modbus.
  • wM-Bus: one-way radio, AES keys per meter, OMS for interoperability.

4.4 BACnet MS/TP

BACnet (ISO 16484-5, ASHRAE 135) is covered in depth in Chapter 05. Its serial variant, MS/TP (Master-Slave/Token-Passing), runs BACnet over RS-485. It is widely used to connect room controllers, VAV boxes and fan coil controllers to a router that leads to BACnet/IP.

Why token passing?

Modbus has one master that asks everybody. BACnet devices are peers: a room controller may need to send a change-of-value notification or read a value from another controller on its own initiative. On a shared half-duplex wire only one device may talk at a time. MS/TP solves it with a token: only the master holding the token may start a conversation. When it is done, or has sent its allowance of frames (Max_Info_Frames), it passes the token to the next master.

ItemValue
Speeds9600, 19200, 38400, 76800 bit/s (later addenda add 57600 and 115200). 38400 and 76800 are common.

Poll For Master

How does a master know who comes next? Each master remembers its "next station". Every so often (every 50 token passes, Npoll) it sends a Poll For Master frame to each address between itself and its next station. A device that has just been connected answers "Reply To Poll For Master" and is inserted into the ring. Addresses above the highest real master are polled up to Max_Master, which is why leaving it at 127 on a bus with masters 1–10 wastes time on every poll cycle.

Common problems

  • Duplicate MAC addresses. Two devices answer the same token; the ring breaks. Symptoms are intermittent, which makes it hard to find.
  • Mixed baud rates. Some devices auto-baud, some do not. One wrong device disturbs everyone.
  • Too many devices. Each master adds token time. 30–60 devices per trunk at 38400 or 76800 is a common design limit, well below the RS-485 maximum, to keep response times usable.
  • Physical layer. Everything in 4.1 applies. MS/TP is more sensitive than Modbus because every device transmits, not only the master.
A typical office floor: 40 fan coil controllers on two MS/TP trunks at 76800 bit/s, MAC 1–20 on each, Max_Master set to 25. Each trunk ends at a BACnet router (MS/TP to BACnet/IP) in the floor's riser cupboard. The supervisor reads all fan coils through the router as if they were IP devices.

A trunk has masters at MAC 1–12 and Max_Master = 127. What is the effect?

  • MS/TP = BACnet over RS-485 with a token so that peers can share the wire.
  • Masters 0–127. Poll For Master lets new devices join.
  • Set Max_Master close to the highest real address. Unique MACs, one baud rate.

4.5 KNX

KNX (ISO/IEC 14543-3, EN 50090) grew out of EIB, launched in 1990. It is the dominant room-automation bus in Europe for lighting, blinds, heating valves, presence detectors and push buttons. Hundreds of manufacturers make certified devices, and all are configured with one tool, ETS.

Decentralised by design

There is no central controller. A push button sends a telegram "group 1/0/1 = ON". Every actuator that has been programmed to listen to group 1/0/1 switches. If any single device fails, the rest keep working. Logic can live in the devices themselves (a presence detector switching a light, a thermostat driving a valve).

Media

MediumDetails
TP (twisted pair)9600 bit/s, ~30 V DC bus voltage from a KNX power supply with choke, power and data on the same green cable. By far the most common.

Topology and individual addresses

A TP line holds up to 64 devices per segment (up to 256 with line repeaters), max 1000 m of cable, with distance limits between devices and to the power supply (700 m and 350 m). Up to 15 lines join a main line through line couplers to form an area; up to 15 areas join the backbone. Today the backbone and often the main lines are IP. Each device has an individual address area.line.device, e.g. 1.2.15, used for programming and diagnostics, not for normal operation.

Group addresses: the heart of KNX

Normal operation uses group addresses, 16-bit numbers that stand for a function, not a device. In the common three-level style they are written main/middle/sub (5 + 3 + 8 bits: 0–31 / 0–7 / 0–255). A typical scheme: main = floor or trade, middle = function, sub = room. Each value has a datapoint type (DPT): DPT 1.001 on/off (1 bit), DPT 5.001 percentage (1 byte), DPT 9.001 temperature (2-byte float).

Try it: who listens to which group?

Bus access

KNX TP uses CSMA/CA with bitwise arbitration. A logical 0 is dominant (the transmitter pulls the bus), a 1 is recessive. If two devices start together, the one that sends a 1 while the bus shows 0 notices and backs off; the winning telegram is not damaged. Telegram priority bits in the header make system and alarm telegrams win.

A 40-room hotel: each room has a KNX room controller, a 4-channel switch actuator, a blind actuator and a key-card switch. Group addresses follow floor/function/room: 2/0/14 is "room 214 main light", 2/4/14 "room 214 occupancy". A KNX IP router per floor joins the lines over the building network, and the BMS reads occupancy and temperatures through a KNXnet/IP interface.

You replace a broken switch actuator with a new one of the same model. What must ETS download to it?

  • KNX is decentralised: devices talk to group addresses, not to each other.
  • Individual address area.line.device; group address main/middle/sub.
  • TP: 9600 bit/s, bus-powered, 64 devices per segment. IP for backbones.
  • Every project lives in the ETS file. Keep it safe; without it, changes are painful.

4.6 DALI and DALI-2

DALI (Digital Addressable Lighting Interface, IEC 62386) is the standard digital interface for LED drivers and ballasts. Instead of a 1–10 V wire per channel, every luminaire sits on a two-wire bus and is addressed individually.

The bus

  • Two wires, polarity-free, can run in the same cable as mains (it is not SELV-rated, so treat it as mains).
  • A bus power supply provides about 16 V (9.5–22.5 V allowed) and at most 250 mA.
  • 1200 bit/s, Manchester (bi-phase) coded. Max 300 m and 2 V drop; 1.5 mm² cable is the usual choice.
  • Free topology: line, star, tree. No termination.

Addressing: 64, 16, 16

ConceptCountStored inMeaning
Short address64 (0–63)each driverOne driver, set during commissioning by random-address search
Group16 (0–15)each driverA driver can be in any number of groups
Scene16 (0–15)each driverA stored level per scene; "MASK" (255) means not part of it
Broadcast1—All drivers on the line

The key point: groups and scenes live in the drivers. "Recall scene 2" is one telegram; every driver looks up its own stored level for scene 2 and fades to it at the same time. Scenes are therefore perfectly synchronised, whatever the number of luminaires.

The forward frame

A command from the controller is a 16-bit forward frame: an address byte and a data byte. The address byte encodes who is addressed:

  • 0AAAAAAS: short address A (0–63)
  • 100GGGGS: group G (0–15)
  • 1111111S: broadcast

The last bit S selects the meaning of the data byte. S = 0: direct arc power level (0–254). S = 1: a command, e.g. 0x00 OFF, 0x05 RECALL MAX LEVEL, 0x10–0x1F GO TO SCENE 0–15. A driver can answer queries with an 8-bit backward frame.

Try it: groups and scenes

Logarithmic dimming

Arc power levels 1–254 map logarithmically to 0.1–100 % light output. The eye perceives brightness roughly logarithmically, so equal steps in level look like equal steps in brightness. Level 0 is off.

DALI-2 and D4i

The original DALI only standardised control gear (drivers). Sensors and buttons were proprietary. DALI-2 adds certified control devices (IEC 62386-103) and input devices: push buttons (part 301), occupancy sensors (303), light sensors (304). They use 24-bit forward frames so they do not collide with driver commands. Device types extend the gear: DT6 for LED, DT8 for colour (tunable white, RGBW). D4i adds energy and diagnostic data inside the luminaire (power, operating hours, failures).

An open-plan office floor: four DALI lines of 50 drivers each, a DALI-2 multi-master application controller per line, DALI-2 presence and daylight sensors in the ceiling. Desks near the windows dim automatically. A DALI-to-BACnet or DALI-to-MQTT gateway exposes groups and failure status to the BMS, which only sends scenes and reads lamp failures, never individual levels.

A DALI line has 70 drivers. What is the problem?

  • DALI: two wires, 1200 bit/s, bus-powered, free topology.
  • 64 short addresses, 16 groups, 16 scenes per line; groups and scenes live in the drivers.
  • DALI-2 standardises sensors and buttons; DT8 adds colour, D4i adds diagnostics.

4.7 LON (legacy)

LonWorks (LON, Local Operating Network) was developed by Echelon in the late 1980s and standardised as ISO/IEC 14908 (also EN 14908). Through the 1990s and 2000s it competed with BACnet for HVAC and was popular in Europe for room automation, lighting and building services. New projects rarely choose it, but many buildings still run on it, and you need to know enough to keep it alive or migrate it.

How it works

  • Neuron chip: devices historically used a dedicated processor running the LonTalk protocol stack (later also other chips with the stack in software).
  • FT-10 free topology: 78 kbit/s twisted pair, polarity-free, any topology (line, star, ring, tree), with one terminator for free topology or two for bus topology. Up to about 500 m in free topology, 2700 m as a bus.
  • Peer to peer: devices exchange network variables directly once they are "bound" together, similar in spirit to KNX group addresses.
  • SNVTs (Standard Network Variable Types): standardised types such as SNVT_temp_p (temperature) or SNVT_switch, which made multi-vendor work possible.
  • Engineering: a network database (LNS-based tools) holds every binding and address. Lose the database, and changing the network becomes very hard.

  • LON: ISO/IEC 14908, peer-to-peer network variables, SNVTs, FT-10 free topology at 78 kbit/s.
  • Legacy today: keep, gateway or migrate in stages.
  • The network database is the system. Secure it first.

4.8 OpenTherm and boiler buses (eBUS, OT)

A room thermostat can switch a boiler on and off with a relay. That throws away most of a modern condensing boiler's efficiency, because it then always fires at a fixed flow temperature. Boiler buses let the controller ask for how much heat, and read back what the boiler is doing.

OpenTherm

OpenTherm (OT), maintained by the OpenTherm Association, is a point-to-point protocol between one master (room controller, BMS interface) and one slave (boiler, heat pump). It uses the existing two thermostat wires, polarity-free, and powers the thermostat from the boiler.

  • Master → slave: voltage levels (low < 7 V, high 15–18 V).
  • Slave → master: current levels (low 5–9 mA, high 17–23 mA).
  • Manchester coding, 1000 bit/s. The master must send a frame at least once a second; the slave answers within 20–800 ms.
  • If communication stops, the boiler falls back after a timeout, and many boilers then accept the same two wires as a simple on/off contact.

The 32-bit frame

Every message is one 32-bit frame: parity (1 bit, even parity over the whole frame), message type (3), spare (4), Data-ID (8) and data value (16). Data-IDs are standardised: ID 0 is the status exchange, ID 1 is the control setpoint (the flow temperature the master wants), ID 17 is relative modulation level, ID 25 boiler flow temperature. Temperatures use the f8.8 format: a signed 16-bit number divided by 256.

eBUS

eBUS (energy bus) is a German two-wire bus used by several boiler and heat pump makers (Vaillant and Wolf, among others) to link boilers, controllers, mixer modules and solar controllers. Unlike OpenTherm it is a real multi-master bus: 2400 bit/s, about 15–24 V, polarity-free. Devices arbitrate by sending their address byte after a SYN symbol (0xAA); the lower address wins. The framing is open, but most message contents are manufacturer-specific, so integration usually relies on community-decoded message definitions (for example the ebusd project) or the vendor's own gateway.

Other routes into a boiler

  • 0–10 V input: setpoint or burner output demand as an analog signal (see 4.9).
  • Modbus: commercial boilers and cascade controllers often offer Modbus RTU or TCP via an option card.
  • Vendor buses: e.g. proprietary two-wire buses; only the vendor's gateway can talk to them.
A small office with a 30 kW wall-hung condensing boiler: the BMS talks to an OpenTherm gateway (an OT master with Modbus or MQTT on the other side). The BMS sends ID 1 = 48 °C when the weather-compensated curve asks for it, and reads ID 25 (flow), ID 28 (return) and ID 17 (modulation) for trends and fault detection. When the gateway fails, the boiler falls back to its own outdoor-sensor curve.

Why is OpenTherm better than an on/off relay for a condensing boiler?

  • OpenTherm: point-to-point, two wires, 32-bit frames, one frame per second, f8.8 temperatures.
  • ID 1 = control setpoint, ID 25 = flow temperature, ID 17 = modulation.
  • eBUS: multi-master, 2400 bit/s, largely vendor-specific content.

4.9 Analog and dry-contact I/O: 0–10 V, 4–20 mA, relays

Below every bus there is still copper carrying a single value. Analog and binary I/O is simple, universal and needs no configuration, but every wire carries exactly one value and the controller must know the scaling.

SignalTypical useStrengthsWeaknesses
0–10 VValve and damper actuators, EC fans, VFD speedCheap, simple, common in HVACVoltage drop and ground offsets add error; 0 V is indistinguishable from a broken wire
2–10 VSame, with live zeroBelow ~1 V means faultStill sensitive to ground offsets
4–20 mAPressure, CO₂, level, flow transmittersImmune to cable resistance, live zero, long runsNeeds loop supply and compliance voltage
PT1000 / NTCTemperature sensorsPassive, cheapCable resistance adds error (PT1000: ~0.26 °C per ohm)
Dry contactStatus, alarms, enablesSimplest possible signalOne bit per pair; choose NO or NC deliberately

Why current beats voltage on long runs

In a current loop, the same current flows through every part of the series circuit. Cable resistance does not change it, as long as the supply has enough voltage left to push it ("compliance"). A voltage signal is measured against the controller's ground: any current flowing in a shared return wire, or a ground difference between panels, adds directly to the reading.

Relays and dry contacts

A dry contact is a switch with no voltage of its own; the input supplies it. Choose the contact type for the failure you care about:

  • Normally closed (NC) for alarms. A broken wire looks like an alarm, so faults are never silent. Fire, frost and high-pressure trips are wired this way.
  • Normally open (NO) for status. "Pump running" closes when true.
  • Outputs: relays switch the coil of a contactor or a 24 V enable, not the motor directly. Check the contact rating, and use an interposing relay for anything inductive or at mains voltage.

A typical office AHU controller has about 30 I/O points: PT1000 sensors for supply, return, outdoor and frost temperatures; 0–10 V outputs to the heating valve, cooling valve, damper actuators and EC fans; a 4–20 mA duct pressure transmitter; NC contacts for frost thermostat, fire damper end switches and fan faults; NO contacts for fan running; and relay outputs enabling the fans and the heat recovery wheel.

A 0–10 V damper feedback reads 0.4 V too high, and the error grows when the actuators move. Likely cause?

  • 0–10 V is cheap but sensitive to ground offsets. 4–20 mA ignores cable resistance and detects breaks.
  • Give voltage signals their own signal common; never share it with actuator supply current.
  • Wire alarms NC so a broken wire is never silent.
