Chapter 10

Hydronics

Heat is made in one room and used in hundreds of others. Water carries it. This chapter covers the physics of moving heat with water, the pumps and valves that do it, and the hydraulic tricks that keep every room fed without noise, waste or fights between circuits.

10.1 Flow, head, delta T, the hydraulic equation

A hydronic system is a closed loop of water. A heat source warms it, a pump pushes it through pipes, emitters give the heat to the rooms, and the cooler water returns. Three quantities describe what happens:

  • Flow (Q or V̇): how much water moves, in m³/h, l/h or l/s.
  • Temperature difference (ΔT): supply minus return, in kelvin (K). One kelvin of difference equals one °C of difference.
  • Head (H) or differential pressure (Δp): how hard the pump pushes, in metres of water column (m) or kPa. 1 m ≈ 9.81 kPa ≈ 0.1 bar.

The hydraulic equation

The heat carried by water is mass flow times specific heat times temperature difference:

P = ṁ · c · ΔT

For water, c ≈ 4.19 kJ/(kg·K) and 1 m³ weighs about 1000 kg (a bit less when hot). Combine the constants and you get a rule every heating engineer knows by heart:

P [kW] ≈ 1.16 · Q [m³/h] · ΔT [K]

So 1 m³/h of water cooling by 20 K delivers about 23 kW. The same 23 kW at ΔT 5 K needs 4 m³/h, four times the flow. That is the core trade-off of hydronics: a small ΔT means big flow, big pipes and big pumps.

Head and pressure drop

Water flowing through a pipe loses pressure to friction. For turbulent flow the loss rises roughly with the square of the flow: double the flow, about four times the pressure drop. Designers keep pipe friction around 100–200 Pa per metre and velocities below about 0.5–1.0 m/s in pipes inside rooms. Higher speeds whistle and erode valve seats. A pump must supply a head equal to the total pressure drop of the worst path: boiler, pipes, valves, emitter and back.

Valves are described by their Kv value: the flow in m³/h that passes with 1 bar of pressure drop. The relation is Q = Kv · √Δp with Δp in bar. A valve with Kv 2.5 passing 1 m³/h drops (1/2.5)² = 0.16 bar = 16 kPa.

An office floor needs 60 kW of heating. With 70/50 °C radiators (ΔT 20 K) that is 60 / (1.16 × 20) ≈ 2.6 m³/h. A DN40 pipe (about 41 mm inner diameter) carries it at 0.55 m/s. Converted to a heat pump at 45/40 °C (ΔT 5 K), the same load needs 10.3 m³/h. The old riser would run at about 2.2 m/s: noisy, with roughly sixteen times the friction loss. This is one reason heat pump retrofits often need new pipework or bigger emitters.
A BMS trend showing ΔT of 2–3 K on a circuit designed for 20 K means flow is far too high. Energy still reaches the rooms, but the pump wastes electricity and a condensing boiler gets hot return water and stops condensing. "Low ΔT syndrome" is one of the most common findings in an energy audit.

A circuit carries 2 m³/h at 70/50 °C. Roughly how much heat does it deliver?

  • P [kW] ≈ 1.16 × Q [m³/h] × ΔT [K]. Memorise it.
  • Small ΔT means big flow. Low-temperature systems need more water, pipe and pump.
  • Pressure drop grows with flow squared. Kv links valve flow and pressure drop.
  • Trend ΔT in the BMS. It is the quickest health check of a circuit.

10.2 Pumps: fixed, variable, curves

Almost every heating pump in a building is a centrifugal pump: an impeller spins and throws water outward. Small ones with the motor sealed inside the water (wet rotor) are called circulators. Examples are the Grundfos Alpha and Magna, Wilo Stratos and Yonos, and Taco or DAB equivalents. Large plant uses dry-rotor inline pumps with a separate motor and a frequency inverter.

The pump curve and the system curve

A pump does not deliver a fixed flow. Its pump curve shows how much head it can produce at each flow: maximum head at zero flow, falling as flow rises. The pipework has a system curve: the head needed to push a given flow, roughly H = k · Q² in a closed loop. The system runs where the two curves cross: the operating point.

When a control valve closes, k grows and the system curve gets steeper. With a fixed-speed pump the operating point slides left and up: less flow, more head. The remaining open valves see a higher pressure, get more flow than intended and may start to whistle.

The affinity laws

For a centrifugal pump changing speed n, with the same system:

QuantityScales withAt 80 % speedAt 50 % speed
Flow Qn80 %50 %
Head Hn²64 %25 %
Power Pn³51 %12.5 %

The cube law is why variable speed pays. A heating system sits at part load most of the year, so a pump that slows to 70 % of speed uses about a third of full power. In practice the saving is smaller, because motor and drive efficiency fall at low speed and a real system curve has a minimum head. It is still large.

Control modes of modern circulators

ModeWhat the pump holdsUse it for

Constant pressure (Δp-c)The same head at any flow.Underfloor heating, systems where the pipe loss is small compared to the emitter loss.

Small circulators often take a PWM signal (profile A for heating: higher duty means lower speed, profile C for solar: the opposite, but check the data sheet). Larger pumps expose Modbus RTU or BACnet with speed, power, head, flow estimate and fault codes, which a BMS should read rather than only switching the pump on and off.

Since 2015 the EU Ecodesign rules require stand-alone glandless circulators to have an Energy Efficiency Index (EEI) of 0.23 or lower. In practice that means a permanent-magnet (ECM) motor and built-in speed control. Swapping an old 3-speed 90 W circulator for a modern one in Δp-v mode often brings it down to 10–25 W average.
A pump running against closed valves heats the water inside it and can be damaged. Systems where every emitter can close need a minimum-flow path: a bypass valve, one always-open circuit, or a pump mode that drops to minimum speed. Pump "run-on" after a burner stops also needs somewhere to send the water.

A fixed-speed pump serves radiators with TRVs. Half the TRVs close. What happens?

  • The operating point is where the pump curve meets the system curve.
  • Flow ∝ speed, head ∝ speed², power ∝ speed³.
  • Δp-c suits underfloor heating, Δp-v suits radiators with TRVs.
  • Read pump data (power, flow, faults) over the bus, not just run status.

10.3 Hydraulic separators and buffers

Bigger plants have several pumps: a boiler or heat pump pump on the primary side, and one pump per consumer circuit on the secondary side. If they are piped in plain series, they fight. A strong circuit pump can pull water backwards through a weak one, or force flow through a boiler that is off. The fix is to make the pumps hydraulically independent.

The low-loss header (hydraulic separator)

A hydraulic separator, also called a low-loss header or "LLH", is a short, fat vertical tube that joins primary and secondary. Because it is wide (sized for about 0.1–0.2 m/s), the pressure drop between its top and bottom is nearly zero. Each pump only sees its own circuit. Most separators also slow the water enough for air to rise to a vent at the top and dirt to settle into a drain at the bottom. Closely spaced tees on a common pipe do the same job.

What happens inside depends on which side moves more water:

CaseHeader flowEffect
Primary > secondaryDownwardsCircuits get full boiler temperature. Hot water short-circuits back to the boiler, raising its return. Bad for condensing.
Primary = secondaryAlmost noneIdeal, rarely reached for long.
Primary < secondaryUpwardsReturn water mixes into the supply. Circuits get cooler water than the boiler makes. The boiler sees the true cold return.

For condensing boilers and heat pumps, designers aim for primary flow slightly lower than secondary flow, so that return temperature stays low. The BMS should then control the heat source on a sensor in the secondary supply after the header, not on the boiler outlet, because that is the temperature the circuits really get.

Buffer tanks

A buffer tank is a separator with volume. It does three extra jobs:

  • Prevents short cycling. A heat pump or biomass boiler should run at least 10–20 minutes per start. Without enough water, a small load makes it start and stop every few minutes.
  • Covers defrost. Air-source heat pumps reverse briefly to defrost the outdoor coil and take heat from the water.
  • Stores cheap or free heat. Solar thermal, off-peak tariffs, PV surplus.

The energy stored is E [kWh] = 1.16 × V [m³] × ΔT [K]. A 500 l buffer charged by 10 K holds about 5.8 kWh: enough to run a 6 kW load for about an hour, or to give a 12 kW heat pump close to half an hour of runtime. A common rule of thumb is 20–50 l of system volume per kW for heat pumps, but manufacturers' minimum volumes come first.

A 4-pipe buffer used as a separator stratifies: hot at top, cool at bottom. Sensor position matters. A single sensor in the middle of a 1000 l tank can read 45 °C while the top is 60 °C and the bottom 30 °C. Use two or three sensors (top, middle, bottom) and charge on the bottom one.

A condensing boiler pumps 4 m³/h into a separator; the circuits draw 2 m³/h. What does the boiler see?

  • A separator makes primary and secondary pumps independent.
  • Primary > secondary heats the return. Primary < secondary cools the supply.
  • Control the heat source on the secondary supply sensor.
  • Buffers add volume against short cycling and defrost; measure them at several heights.

10.4 Mixing circuits and 3-way valves

One heat source often serves circuits that need different temperatures: radiators at 60 °C, floor heating at 35 °C, an air handler at 70 °C. A mixing circuit lowers the temperature locally by blending hot supply with cooler return water. It is the classic weather-compensated circuit you met in the control theory chapter: outdoor temperature sets a supply setpoint, a PI loop moves the valve.

Mixing versus diverting

ArrangementPortsFlow in circuitFlow at the sourceTypical use
Mixing (in supply)Two inlets, one outletConstant, temperature variesVariesUnderfloor and radiator circuits.
Diverting (in return)One inlet, two outletsVaries through the coilConstantAir handler coils, constant-flow primaries.
Injection2-way valve or small pump into a circuitConstantSmall, variesLarge low-temperature circuits fed from a hot main.
2-way valveOne in, one outVariesVariesVariable-flow systems with variable-speed pumps.

Actuators and signals

Valve actuators come in three main styles:

  • 3-point (floating): two triacs, "open" and "close". The controller pulses them. Runtime is 60–240 s end to end. No position feedback unless the actuator has one, so the controller estimates it.
  • Modulating 0–10 V or 2–10 V: the signal sets a position. Many give a 2–10 V position feedback.
  • Bus: Modbus, BACnet MS/TP or a vendor bus (Belimo MP-Bus, for example) with position, setpoint and diagnostics.

A good valve has authority: its pressure drop when fully open should be a large share (often at least half) of the pressure drop of the part of the circuit whose flow it changes. A valve that is too big does nothing for most of its stroke, then everything in the last 10 %. The PI loop will hunt. Oversized valves are the most common reason a mixing circuit oscillates.

A floor-heating circuit is fed from a boiler at 70 °C. On a mild day the curve asks for 32 °C. With a 30 °C return, the valve sits about 5 % open to port A. Any small movement causes a big swing. Lowering boiler temperature to 50 °C moves the working point to 10 % and makes the loop far calmer. Matching the source temperature to the hottest current demand helps both efficiency and control.
Underfloor circuits need a high-limit safety thermostat (for example 55 °C) wired to stop the circuit pump. It must act directly, not through BMS software. A stuck mixing valve with 70 °C supply can damage screed and floor finishes.

A mixing circuit oscillates ±4 K around setpoint. Valve position swings between 3 % and 15 %. Most likely cause?

  • Mixing valves blend supply and return; circuit flow stays constant.
  • Diverting valves vary coil flow and keep source flow constant.
  • Valve authority matters: oversized valves make loops hunt.
  • Floor circuits need a hard-wired high-limit thermostat.

10.5 Underfloor heating: manifolds, actuators

Underfloor heating (UFH) embeds pipe loops, usually 16–17 mm PE-X or PE-RT, in screed or in dry panels. The whole floor becomes a low-temperature emitter. A typical output is 50–100 W/m², limited by floor surface temperature. European standard EN 1264 sets the usual limits: about 29 °C in occupied zones, 33 °C in bathrooms and 35 °C at perimeter zones.

The manifold

Each room has one or more loops, all starting and ending at a manifold, usually in a cabinet in a corridor.

  • Supply bar with flow meters (rotameters) and balancing valves per loop.
  • Return bar with valve inserts that accept actuators.
  • Air vents, drain/fill cocks, often a thermometer on each bar.
  • Often a mixing unit with its own pump and thermostatic or motorised valve.

Loops are typically kept under about 100–120 m to limit pressure drop. Pipe spacing is 100–200 mm, tighter by outside walls. A loop's design flow is often 1–3 l/min.

Thermoelectric actuators

The loop valves are usually driven by thermoelectric actuators ("heads"). A small heater warms a wax or expansion element that pushes the valve pin. They are slow and simple:

PropertyTypical value
Supply230 V AC or 24 V AC/DC
PowerAbout 1–3 W holding, with an inrush current when cold (matters for 24 V transformer sizing)
DefaultNormally closed (NC) is most common; normally open (NO) exists
Opening timeAbout 2–5 minutes
ControlOn/off, or PWM with a few-minute period; some 0–10 V versions

Because the floor responds over hours, on/off control of the loop valves works well. A room thermostat or a BMS output switches the actuator of that room's loops. A wiring centre (Uponor, Danfoss, Rehau and many others) groups the outputs and often switches the pump off when all loops are closed.

A 120 m² flat has nine loops on one manifold. On a mild day only the bathroom and one bedroom call for heat. The circulator in Δp-c mode slows down and the two open loops keep their design flow of about 2 l/min. With a fixed-speed pump, they would get roughly double and the bathroom loop would whistle at the valve insert.
Screed floors have large time constants: 4–12 hours. A night setback of 4 K usually saves little and causes a cold morning. Most UFH systems run better with a small setback or none, and with weather-compensated supply temperature doing most of the work.

A room thermostat calls for heat but the floor loop stays cold for the first few minutes. Why?

  • UFH is a low-temperature, high-mass emitter: 50–100 W/m², floor ≤ 29 °C in living areas.
  • A manifold balances and switches loops; thermoelectric heads open in minutes.
  • Use Δp-c pump mode so closing loops does not overflow the rest.
  • Slow floors need gentle setbacks and good weather compensation.

10.6 Radiators and TRVs

"Radiator" is a misleading name. Panel radiators give most heat by convection: air warms, rises, and pulls cool air in at the bottom. Only the front panel radiates much. Types include panel radiators (type 11, 21, 22, 33: number of panels and convector fins), column radiators, towel rails, fan convectors and trench heaters.

Output depends on excess temperature

Rated output is measured by EN 442 at 75/65 °C water and 20 °C room: an excess temperature ΔT of 50 K (mean water minus room). At other conditions:

P = Pn · (ΔT / 50)ⁿ, with n ≈ 1.3 for panel radiators

Output falls faster than the temperature drop. At ΔT 25 K (for example 50/40 °C water in a 20 °C room) a radiator gives only about 40 % of its rated output. That is why heat pump conversions often need larger radiators or fan-assisted ones.

Thermostatic radiator valves

A TRV is a self-acting proportional controller. A sensor head with wax or liquid expands as the room warms and pushes the valve towards closed. Its proportional band is usually about 2 K: the valve moves from its design opening to closed over a 2 K rise. No power, no wires, no communication.

  • Pre-setting on the valve body limits the maximum flow. This is how radiator systems are balanced.
  • Electronic TRVs (battery heads using Zigbee, Z-Wave, Thread or proprietary radio, from Danfoss, Eurotronic, Tado, Homematic and others) replace the wax head with a motor and add schedules and remote setpoints.
  • Lockshield valve on the return is the other end of the radiator, used for isolation and sometimes for balancing.
Limits maximum flow with a numbered pre-setting ring
Contains the wax element that senses room temperature
Rated output at ΔT 50 K
Replaced when upgrading to a battery-powered smart controller
Type 22 with two panels and fins
Do not let a BMS fight a TRV. If a smart head and a central room controller both regulate the same room, one will close while the other asks for heat. Pick one owner of the room setpoint. Also check where the sensor sits: a head behind curtains or in a radiator niche reads high and starves the room.

A radiator rated 2000 W at ΔT 50 K runs with 50/40 °C water in a 20 °C room. Roughly what does it give?

  • Radiator output ∝ (excess ΔT)^1.3; rated at ΔT 50 K by EN 442.
  • Halving excess temperature leaves about 40 % of output.
  • A TRV is a local proportional controller with about 2 K band.
  • One owner of each room setpoint; balance via TRV pre-settings.

10.7 Hydronic balancing

Water takes the easiest path. Emitters close to the pump have short pipes and little resistance. Emitters at the end of a long run have a lot. Without balancing, near emitters get too much flow and far ones starve. People then raise the boiler temperature or pump speed until the last room is warm, wasting energy everywhere else.

Hydronic balancing adds resistance (or active control) so each emitter or branch gets its design flow. Many countries require it for new installations or to qualify for subsidies, often citing EN 14336 or national procedures (for example method A/B in Germany).

Tools and valve types

MethodHow it worksProsCons
Pre-set TRVs / lockshieldsSet a fixed resistance per radiator from a calculation.Cheap, no extra parts.Static; needs a calculation and an honest pipe survey.
Static balancing valvesCalibrated valve with two test points. A meter reads Δp; the known Kv gives flow. Products: IMI TA STAD, Oventrop Hydrocontrol, Danfoss MSV and others.Measurable, documented flows.Correct only at design conditions; needs a commissioning visit.
Differential pressure controllersHold constant Δp across a branch or riser (Danfoss ASV, IMI STAP).Branches stop affecting each other; TRVs work quietly.Extra cost and pressure drop.
Pressure-independent control valves (PICV)Control valve and Δp regulator in one body. Max flow is set directly.Flow stays right as other loads change; full valve authority.Needs a minimum Δp (often 15–30 kPa) to work; more expensive.

Commissioning procedure, in short

  1. Calculate heat load per room and the design flow for each emitter or branch.
  2. Flush and vent the system. Balancing a system full of air or dirt gives wrong readings.
  3. Open all control valves fully (TRV heads off or in "max").
  4. Set and measure branches from the far end back (proportional or compensated methods), or simply set PICVs to their flows.
  5. Find the index circuit: the path with the highest resistance. Set the pump so that circuit just gets design flow, no more.
  6. Record results. A BMS should store the commissioned flows and pump setpoint as reference values.
After balancing an 80-radiator school, the circulator setpoint dropped from 6 m to 3.5 m and boiler supply from 75 °C to 62 °C, while the far classrooms finally reached 20 °C. Results like 5–15 % heating energy savings are often reported, though they depend heavily on how bad the starting point was.
Turning the pump up is not balancing. It raises flow everywhere, near emitters get even more, and the ratio between near and far stays roughly the same.

What is the main advantage of a PICV over a static balancing valve plus a 2-way control valve?

  • Without balancing, near emitters overflow and far ones starve.
  • Static valves are right only at design; Δp controllers and PICVs adapt.
  • Balance first, then set the pump for the index circuit.
  • A higher pump setpoint is never a fix for poor balancing.

10.8 Pressure, expansion, water quality

A closed heating system is a sealed vessel of water that changes volume with temperature. Water heated from 10 °C to 70 °C expands by about 2.3 %; to 90 °C, about 3.6 %. A 1000 l system grows by 23–36 l. Water does not compress, so without somewhere to go, the pressure would rise until the safety valve opens.

Pressures that matter

The top of the system must stay above atmospheric pressure, or air is drawn in through vents and fittings. Pump inlets also need enough pressure to avoid cavitation, especially with hot water.

Membrane expansion vessels

A steel vessel with a rubber membrane: nitrogen or air on one side, system water on the other. As the system heats, water flows in and compresses the gas. Sizing follows Boyle's law. In the method used by EN 12828 and most vendor tools:

Vₙ = (Vₑ + V_wr) · (pₑ + 1) / (pₑ − p₀)

Vₑ is the expansion volume, V_wr a water reserve (often 0.5 % of system volume, at least 3 l), and pressures are gauge values in bar (the "+1" converts to absolute).

What a BMS should watch

  • System pressure with a 4–20 mA or 0–10 V transmitter. Low pressure alarm (for example below fill pressure minus 0.3 bar) and high alarm close to the safety valve.
  • Pressure trend: a slow daily fall means a leak or a vessel that lost its pre-charge. A large swing between cold and hot means the vessel is too small or flat.
  • Make-up water meter: every litre of fresh water brings oxygen and minerals. Automatic fill units should be metered and limited.

Water quality

Fresh water carries oxygen and hardness. Oxygen corrodes steel into black sludge (magnetite) that blocks valves and wears pumps. Hardness forms limescale on the hottest surfaces: heat exchangers. Guidelines such as German VDI 2035, British BS 7593 and manufacturers' rules set limits. Typical points:

ParameterTypical guidance
pHAbout 8.2–10 for steel/copper systems; usually ≤ 8.5 when aluminium heat exchangers are present
HardnessFill water softened or demineralised in larger systems; limits depend on power and volume
ConductivityLow-salt operation (for example < 100 µS/cm) reduces corrosion
OxygenKept low by avoiding make-up water and air ingress

Protection devices: magnetic dirt separators, microbubble deaerators, inhibitor dosing, and in cooling or outdoor circuits glycol antifreeze. Glycol lowers specific heat (by roughly 10–15 % at 30–40 % concentration) and raises viscosity, so flow and pump head must be recalculated.

A plant room trend shows system pressure at 1.8 bar cold, 2.9 bar hot, and the safety valve set at 3 bar drips every afternoon. The vessel's pre-charge has leaked down to zero, so it holds almost no expansion. Recharging the gas side to design pre-charge (with the vessel isolated and drained on the water side) fixes it.
Never check or adjust pre-charge with the vessel connected and pressurised. You will only measure system pressure. Isolate, drain the water side, then measure.

The pressure of a sealed system falls by about 0.1 bar every few days, and an auto-fill keeps topping it up. What is the main long-term risk?

  • Water expands 2–4 % when heated; the expansion vessel absorbs it.
  • Pre-charge ≈ static head + 0.2–0.3 bar; fill a little above pre-charge.
  • Trend system pressure and meter make-up water.
  • Treat fill water; oxygen and hardness destroy heat exchangers and pumps.
