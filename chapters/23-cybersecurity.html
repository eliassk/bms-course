Chapter 23

OT cybersecurity

Building systems were designed for closed networks and trusted technicians. Today they sit on IP, talk to clouds and are reachable by vendors from anywhere. This chapter shows how attacks really reach a boiler or an air handler, and the engineering habits that stop them: zones, conduits, least privilege, patching and honest thinking about dependencies.

23.1 Threats to building systems

OT (operational technology) is hardware and software that changes the physical world: controllers, actuators, drives, meters. IT protects data; OT protects processes. The classic CIA triad (confidentiality, integrity, availability) is still used, but the order flips. In a building, availability and integrity come first: a leaked temperature log is embarrassing, a frozen coil or a disabled smoke damper is dangerous.

Who attacks buildings, and why

  • Opportunistic scanners. Bots sweep the whole IPv4 space in under an hour. Search engines such as Shodan and Censys index devices that answer on 47808/udp (BACnet), 502/tcp (Modbus) or a vendor web UI. Researchers have repeatedly counted thousands to tens of thousands of BMS devices reachable this way.
  • Ransomware crews. Usually after IT, but a flat network lets them encrypt the BMS server too. The building then runs "blind" on local controller logic, if it was designed to.
  • Stepping stone. The well-known 2013 Target breach is widely reported to have started with credentials stolen from an HVAC contractor. The BMS was not the goal; it was the door.
  • Insiders and mistakes. A technician's laptop with malware, a shared password, an accidental write to the wrong Modbus register. Most incidents are not movie hackers.

A common finding in audits: a BMS web interface published on the internet with a port forward "so the service company can check alarms". It runs a 2015 firmware with a known authentication bypass. No exploit skill is needed; a public script works.
"Nobody wants to hack our heating" is not a threat model. Scanners do not care what you are. And an attacker who gets into the BMS often gets a foothold on a network that also carries the access control system and the cameras.

Why is classic BACnet/IP or Modbus TCP dangerous when reachable from an untrusted network?

  • In OT, availability and integrity matter more than confidentiality.
  • Most attacks are opportunistic or use the BMS as a stepping stone.
  • Typical entries: exposed services, vendor access, compromised IT, infected laptops.

23.2 IEC 62443 basics

IEC 62443 is the international series of standards for industrial automation and control system (IACS) security. It was written for factories, but building automation fits it well, and it is increasingly referenced in tenders and in EU regulation discussions. It is split into four groups:

GroupAudienceKey parts
1 Generaleveryoneconcepts, terms, models
2 Policies and proceduresasset owner2-1 security programme, 2-3 patch management, 2-4 service provider requirements
3 Systemsystem integrator3-2 risk assessment, zones and conduits; 3-3 system security requirements and SLs
4 Componentproduct vendor4-1 secure development lifecycle; 4-2 component requirements

The split makes responsibility explicit. A vendor can certify a controller to 4-2, but the integrator still has to design zones (3-2), and the owner still has to run a patch process (2-3). A certified product in a flat network is not a secure system.

Zones and conduits

A zone is a group of assets with the same security needs. A conduit is the controlled path between zones: every allowed flow must go through one, and the conduit defines what is allowed. Click a zone or conduit.

Security levels

A security level (SL) describes which attacker a zone must resist. 3-2 sets a target (SL-T) per zone from risk assessment. 3-3 and 4-2 describe what a system or component can achieve (SL-C). The gap between SL-T and what is achieved (SL-A) is closed with compensating controls such as a firewall in front of a weak device.

LevelProtects againstBuilding example
SL 0no specific requirementisolated test bench
SL 1casual or accidental misusea technician writing to the wrong point
SL 2intentional attack with simple means, low resources, generic skillsscript kiddie with a public exploit, opportunistic ransomware
SL 3sophisticated means, moderate resources, IACS-specific skillscriminal group that studies BACnet and targets a hospital
SL 4sophisticated means, extended resources (state level)critical infrastructure; rare for commercial buildings

The standard defines seven foundational requirements (FR): identification and authentication, use control, system integrity, data confidentiality, restricted data flow, timely response to events, and resource availability. An SL is really a vector of seven values, one per FR, though people often quote a single number.

Try it: pick a target level

A legacy controller can only reach SL-C 1, but its zone needs SL-T 2. What does IEC 62443 suggest?

  • IEC 62443 splits duty: owner (part 2), integrator (part 3), vendor (part 4).
  • Zones group assets with the same needs; conduits are the only allowed paths.
  • SL 1–4 rise from accidents to state-level attackers; most buildings target SL 2, safety zones higher.

23.3 Segmentation and firewalls

Segmentation turns the zone model into network reality. VLANs (Chapter 03) separate broadcast domains, but a VLAN alone is not a security boundary: if the router between VLANs allows everything, the attacker just routes. The boundary is the firewall policy on the inter-VLAN router or a dedicated firewall.

Rules that work

  • Default deny. The last rule drops everything. Each allow rule has an owner and a reason.
  • Specific source, destination and port. "BMS server → field VLAN, 47808/udp" not "OT → OT any".
  • Direction matters. Stateful firewalls allow replies automatically. Allow the side that initiates, not both ways.
  • Prefer outbound from OT. A gateway that publishes to a broker in the DMZ needs no inbound hole.
  • Log denies. A controller suddenly trying to reach the internet is a strong alarm signal.

Try it: rule tester

A small policy, evaluated top to bottom, first match wins. Pick a flow and see which rule decides.

A typical mid-size layout: VLAN 10 office, VLAN 20 OT DMZ, VLAN 30 BMS servers, VLAN 40 HVAC controllers, VLAN 50 lighting gateways, VLAN 60 cameras. The firewall has about 15–30 allow rules in total. Anything above 100 usually means rules nobody understands any more.
Watch for dual-homed devices: a BMS server with one NIC in the office VLAN and one in the field VLAN bypasses the firewall completely. The same goes for a cheap 4G router a vendor left in the control cabinet.

With a stateful firewall, to let the BMS server poll controllers, you should allow…

  • A VLAN separates; the firewall policy protects.
  • Default deny, specific rules, allow the initiator only, log the drops.
  • Dual-homed hosts and stray modems silently defeat segmentation.

23.4 Secrets, accounts, least privilege

Buildings are full of default and shared credentials: admin/admin on gateways, one "service" account used by five companies, an API token pasted into a wiki page. Each one is a skeleton key that outlives the people who know it.

Accounts

  • Personal accounts for every human. Shared accounts make audit logs useless.
  • Service accounts for machines, each with only the rights its job needs, and no interactive login.
  • MFA on everything reachable from outside the OT zones, especially remote access.
  • Joiner / mover / leaver. When a contractor's contract ends, accounts end the same day.
  • Break-glass account: one sealed emergency login, stored offline, with an alarm when used.

Secrets

Secrets are passwords, API tokens, TLS private keys, MQTT credentials. Rules:

  • MUST NOT live in source control or images. Use a secret store or environment injected at deploy time.
  • One secret per consumer, so you can revoke one integration without breaking all.
  • Rotate on staff change and after any suspected leak. Know in advance how long a rotation takes.
  • Change every factory default at commissioning. Record it in the handover checklist.

Least privilege: role matrix

Pick a role. A good matrix has few ticks per row, and only the engineer row can change logic.

Try it: how strong is that password?

Entropy is length × log₂(alphabet), assuming random choice. Human-chosen passwords are far weaker than this number suggests.

An MQTT broker in a building can give each gateway its own user and an ACL: the lighting gateway may publish only to lighting/# and subscribe only to lighting/+/set. A compromised gateway can then not spoof boiler states.
Removing a technician's account on the BMS is not enough if the same password is also on the gateways, the router and the vendor portal. Shared secrets multiply the leaver problem.

Five service companies share one "service" login. What is the main problem?

  • Personal accounts for people, scoped service accounts for machines.
  • Secrets out of code, one per consumer, rotated on change.
  • Least privilege: most users read, few command, very few change logic.

23.5 Patching and updates

In IT, "patch fast" is the default. In OT, a patch can stop a plant, change control behaviour or brick a controller whose vendor only tested one firmware path. Still, unpatched systems are where most real compromises start. The answer is a risk-based patch process (IEC 62443-2-3 describes one).

The process

  1. Inventory. You cannot patch what you do not know: model, firmware, location, owner.
  2. Monitor advisories. Vendor PSIRT pages, national CERTs, the CISA ICS advisories and the CISA KEV (Known Exploited Vulnerabilities) catalogue.
  3. Assess. CVSS score is a start, not the answer. Ask: is it exploited in the wild? Is the device reachable from where attackers can be?
  4. Test. On a spare controller or a staging VM. Check the control sequence still runs.
  5. Deploy in a window. Summer for heating plant, winter for chillers. Have a rollback image.
  6. Or mitigate. If you cannot patch, tighten the conduit, disable the service, watch it.

Patch risk vs downtime

Cumulative risk of compromise grows with each day a known-exploited flaw stays open. Patching costs a fixed planned outage. Move the sliders.

A vendor publishes an advisory for a building controller web server: unauthenticated remote code execution, CVSS 9.8. The controllers sit in a field VLAN reachable only from the BMS server. The owner blocks the web port at the conduit the same day (mitigation), then flashes the fix during a planned maintenance night three weeks later.
Auto-update on OT devices is a double-edged tool. A cloud-pushed firmware that changes a default or reboots a gateway at 09:00 on a Monday is an outage you did not schedule. Prefer staged rollouts you control.

Two flaws: A is CVSS 9.8 on an isolated controller; B is CVSS 7.5, listed in CISA KEV, on an internet-facing VPN appliance. Patch first?

  • Inventory first; watch vendor and CERT advisories and the KEV list.
  • Prioritise by exploitation and reachability, not by CVSS alone.
  • When you cannot patch now, mitigate at the conduit and plan a window.

23.6 Vendor and cloud dependency risk

Modern buildings depend on parties you do not control: the vendor's remote access service, a cloud dashboard, a licence server, a mobile app backend. Each one is both a security risk (a door into your network) and an availability risk (a function that disappears when they do).

Remote access done properly

  • Access ends at a jump host in the DMZ, never directly on a controller.
  • Personal accounts with MFA per technician, not per company.
  • Off by default; enabled per session by the owner, time-limited, ideally recorded.
  • No vendor-installed always-on tunnels or 4G routers in panels.
  • Contract clauses: notification of breaches, patch support period, return of credentials at contract end.

Supply chain

Firmware, libraries and engineering tools come from vendors. Ask for a SBOM (software bill of materials) so you know when a library flaw affects you. The EU Cyber Resilience Act will require manufacturers of connected products to handle vulnerabilities and provide security updates; its main obligations phase in over the coming years, so check the current dates.

Dependency map: what fails when?

Switch off external services and see which building functions still work. A sound design keeps core control local.

Several consumer and prosumer device makers have ended cloud services for older products over the years, leaving devices that only worked through that cloud unusable or limited. In a building that means a heating zone or a door lock that silently stops being controllable.
"Cloud-managed" firewalls and switches are convenient, but check what happens when the management cloud is unreachable or the subscription lapses. Some keep forwarding with the last config, some restrict features.

Which design best limits vendor dependency risk?

  • Every external service is both a door and a single point of failure.
  • Remote access: through a jump host, personal MFA accounts, off by default.
  • Keep core control local; ask vendors for SBOMs and support periods.
