Chapter 12

Cooling and heat pumps

One machine moves heat uphill: from a cold place to a warm one. Point it one way and it cools; point it the other and it heats. This chapter explains the refrigeration cycle, the main heat pump and cooling system types, and what a BMS should read, write and leave alone.

12.1 The refrigeration cycle

Heat flows from hot to cold on its own. To move it the other way you need work. Every vapour-compression machine (a fridge, a split air conditioner, a chiller, a heat pump) does this with the same four parts and one trick: a liquid boils at a temperature set by its pressure. Lower the pressure and the refrigerant boils cold, so it can absorb heat from cold air or water. Raise the pressure and it condenses hot, so it can give that heat away to something warmer.

PartWhat entersWhat leavesJob
CompressorLow-pressure vapour, slightly superheatedHigh-pressure, hot gasAdds work. Raises pressure, and with it the condensing temperature.
CondenserHot gasHigh-pressure liquid, slightly subcooledRejects heat to the sink (outdoor air, heating water).
Expansion deviceWarm high-pressure liquidCold liquid/vapour mixDrops pressure. Meters flow. Usually an electronic expansion valve (EEV).
EvaporatorCold two-phase mixLow-pressure vapourAbsorbs heat from the source (room air, outdoor air, brine).

The numbers make it concrete. With R32 in a heat pump heating a floor at 35 °C on a 7 °C day, the evaporator might run at about 0 °C, which is roughly 8 bar absolute. The condenser runs at about 40 °C, roughly 25 bar. The compressor discharge gas can be 70–90 °C. These are typical, not fixed: the controller moves both pressures all the time.

Walk through the cycle

Energy balance and COP

Energy is conserved: heat out of the condenser equals heat in at the evaporator plus compressor work, Q_hot = Q_cold + W. The ratio of useful heat to electrical work is the coefficient of performance. In heating, COP = Q_hot / W. In cooling, the useful effect is Q_cold, and the ratio is usually called EER (energy efficiency ratio). A machine that absorbs 6 kW from outdoor air while the compressor draws 2 kW delivers 8 kW of heat: COP 4. The same machine in cooling would have EER 3.

On the p–h chart, the horizontal lengths are enthalpy changes per kg of refrigerant. That is why engineers love the chart: you can read COP as a ratio of lengths.

Reversing: the four-way valve

A reversible heat pump has a four-way reversing valve after the compressor. It swaps which heat exchanger gets hot gas. In heating mode the indoor coil is the condenser. In cooling mode the outdoor coil is. The same valve is used for reverse-cycle defrost: the unit briefly runs in cooling mode to melt ice off the outdoor coil.

A pressure or temperature reading from inside the refrigerant circuit is diagnostic data. It is useful for trends and fault detection. It is not a control point for the BMS. The unit's own controller owns the compressor, EEV and fans.

A heat pump draws 2.5 kW of electricity and delivers 10 kW of heat. How much heat does it take from the outdoor air?

  • Pressure sets the boiling temperature. The compressor creates a hot side and a cold side.
  • Q_hot = Q_cold + W. COP (heating) and EER (cooling) are ratios of heat to work.
  • A four-way valve reverses the cycle for cooling and for defrost.
  • Refrigerant choice drives safety rules, pressures and regulation.

12.2 Air-source and ground-source heat pumps

The source is where the heat comes from. The sink is where it goes. The single biggest factor in efficiency is the temperature lift between them. The theoretical best (Carnot) COP for heating is T_hot / (T_hot − T_cold) with temperatures in kelvin. Real machines reach roughly 45–60 % of that, and the refrigerant must run a few kelvin colder than the source and hotter than the sink to move heat across the coils.

Explore the lift

Two lessons fall out of the chart. First, every degree of flow temperature costs about 2–3 % of COP. A floor at 35 °C is far better than radiators at 55 °C. Second, the ground source is flat: the ground at 50–100 m depth stays near the annual mean air temperature, so the heat pump has a warm source on the coldest day, when the heat is needed most.

Terms you will see on data sheets

  • A7/W35: air 7 °C in, water 35 °C out. B0/W35: brine 0 °C, water 35 °C. Ratings per EN 14511.
  • SCOP: seasonal COP over a reference climate (EN 14825). Better for comparison than a single-point COP.
  • Bivalent point: the outdoor temperature below which the heat pump alone cannot cover the load, so a backup (electric heater, boiler) helps.
  • Inverter: variable-speed compressor. Modulates, say, 25–100 % instead of cycling on and off.
  • Monoblock vs split: monoblock keeps all refrigerant outdoors and sends water inside; split runs refrigerant pipes to an indoor unit.
A typical retrofit: a 12 kW air-to-water monoblock on an older house with radiators. With a fixed 55 °C flow it measured a seasonal COP under 3. After the installer enabled weather compensation (flow falls to about 35 °C at +10 °C outdoors) and opened all room valves so the heat pump saw a steady load, the measured figure rose to around 3.5. The machine did not change. The flow temperature did.
Short cycling kills efficiency and compressors. If the minimum compressor output is higher than the building load on a mild day, the unit starts and stops many times per hour. Check minimum modulation, add system volume (buffer or open circuits), and do not let room thermostats close every circuit at once.

Why does a ground-source heat pump often beat an air-source one most clearly in January?

  • Lift drives COP. Lower flow temperatures pay back directly.
  • Air source is cheap to install but swings with the weather and needs defrost.
  • Ground source is stable and can cool passively, at a higher install cost.
  • Compare SCOP, not single-point COP.

12.3 VRF/VRV systems

Variable refrigerant flow (VRF; Daikin's trade name is VRV) connects one outdoor unit, or a group of them, to many indoor units through refrigerant pipes. Each indoor unit has its own EEV and modulates its flow. A single system can serve dozens of indoor units and pipe runs well over 100 m. It is common in offices, hotels and retail, where every room wants its own control.

Two families

  • Heat pump (2-pipe, cooling or heating). The whole system is in one mode. A room that wants the other mode must wait.
  • Heat recovery (cooling and heating at once). Branch selector boxes (or a central branch controller) route hot gas to heating units and liquid to cooling units. Heat removed from one room heats another. Daikin and others use three pipes. Mitsubishi Electric's R2 series does it with two pipes and a branch controller.

Why heat recovery saves energy

In spring and autumn, a building often has south rooms that need cooling and north rooms that need heating. A heat recovery system moves heat between them through the refrigerant. When loads balance, the outdoor unit only covers the difference plus losses. Manufacturers quote combined efficiencies well above a single-mode COP in these conditions; the real gain depends on how often loads overlap.

Design rules that matter

TopicTypical constraint
Pipe lengthTotal, longest run, and height difference between indoor units are all limited by the manufacturer.
Connection ratioSum of indoor capacities, often 50–130 % of outdoor capacity, because not every room peaks at once.
Refrigerant in occupied roomsEN 378 / ISO 5149 limit how much refrigerant may leak into the smallest room. Large systems may need leak detectors, shut-off valves or ventilation.
ControlsProprietary bus (for example Daikin DIII-NET, Mitsubishi M-NET). The BMS reaches it through a gateway.
A small hotel room served by a large VRF system is the classic safety trap. If the whole charge, say 30 kg, could leak into one 20 m² room, the concentration limit is exceeded. Designers split systems, add detection with isolation valves, or choose a water-based system instead.
A typical office floor: one 45 kW heat recovery outdoor unit, 18 cassette units, four branch selector boxes, a central touch controller and a BACnet gateway. The BMS reads each unit's on/off, mode, setpoint, room temperature and fault code, and writes on/off and setpoint per schedule. It does not touch EEV positions.

In a 2-pipe heat pump VRF in cooling mode, a north meeting room asks for heat. What happens?

  • VRF: one outdoor unit, many indoor units, refrigerant to every room.
  • Heat pump VRF picks one mode. Heat recovery VRF serves both and moves heat between rooms.
  • Refrigerant charge in small rooms is a safety design issue.
  • Integration is via a vendor gateway, not direct control of the circuit.

12.4 Chillers and fan coils

A chiller runs the refrigeration cycle and cools water instead of air. The chilled water is pumped to fan coil units (FCUs), air handling unit coils, chilled beams or cooled ceilings. Water carries a lot of heat per litre and is safe in occupied rooms, so this scales from one floor to a campus.

The one formula

Q [kW] = flow [l/s] × 4.19 × ΔT [K]. One litre per second of water warming by 6 K carries about 25 kW. A classic design is 6/12 °C (supply/return). Chilled beams and cooled ceilings use higher temperatures, such as 14/17 °C, to stay above the room dew point. A higher supply temperature also raises chiller EER, by very roughly 2–4 % per kelvin.

This is low ΔT syndrome, one of the most common faults in chilled water plants. Causes include three-way valves that bypass cold water, fouled coils, valves that are oversized or stuck open, and supply setpoints set too low. The fix is mostly in hydraulics and control, which is why the BMS should trend supply and return temperatures and flow.

Fan coils: 2-pipe vs 4-pipe

A 2-pipe FCU has one coil. The whole building switches between chilled and hot water by season (changeover). A 4-pipe FCU has a cooling coil and a heating coil with separate valves, so each room can choose. The room controller sequences: heating valve, deadband, cooling valve, with fan speed following demand. Fans are 3-speed or EC with 0–10 V. Condensate is collected in a drip tray, often with a pump and a float switch.

Chilled surfaces below the dew point drip water. Chilled ceilings and beams need a dew point or condensation sensor that raises the supply temperature or closes the valve. Open windows in humid weather are the usual trigger.
A 4-pipe FCU room controller on Modbus or BACnet typically exposes: room temperature, effective setpoint, occupancy, fan speed, heating and cooling valve positions, window contact, condensate alarm and filter hours. A central "season changeover" and "chilled water available" signal come from the plant.

A chilled water loop is designed for 6 K ΔT but runs at 3 K. The pumps are at full speed. What is the likely result?

  • Q = flow × 4.19 × ΔT. Protect the design ΔT.
  • Higher chilled water temperatures improve EER and avoid condensation.
  • 2-pipe FCUs change over by season; 4-pipe FCUs let each room choose.
  • Trend supply, return and flow to catch low ΔT early.

12.5 Integration with the BMS

Heat pumps, chillers and VRF systems come with their own controllers. They protect the compressor, manage oil return, defrost and refrigerant flow. The BMS sits above this. Its job is to say when, how much and to what setpoint, and to watch how well the machine performs.

SG Ready: four states with two contacts

SG Ready is a German label (from the heat pump association BWP) that many European heat pumps support. Two digital inputs give four operating states. A BMS or energy manager can use it to soak up PV surplus or respect a grid signal.

Measure performance, not just state

A heat meter on the heat pump output and an electricity meter on its supply give the real COP. Logged every 15 minutes and summed per day, this is the single most useful KPI. A drop from 4 to 3 at the same weather points to a fault: a dirty coil, low refrigerant, a stuck valve, or a setpoint someone raised.

Do not build a BMS loop that fights the unit's own loop. If the heat pump modulates its compressor to hold 35 °C flow and the BMS also pulses enable on and off to hold a room temperature, you get short cycling. Pick one controller per variable: the BMS moves the setpoint, the unit holds it.
A common supervisory sequence for a chiller plant: the BMS enables chillers by time schedule and outdoor temperature, resets the chilled water setpoint up from 6 °C to 10 °C as valve positions show low demand, stages chillers on load, trends ΔT, and raises an alarm if the fault relay closes or the supply temperature stays 2 K above setpoint for 15 minutes.

You want the heat pump to use more PV surplus at midday. Which approach fits the layering best?

  • The unit controller protects the machine. The BMS decides when, how much and what setpoint.
  • Prefer local interfaces: hard-wired, Modbus, BACnet, vendor gateways.
  • Measure COP with meters. It is the best health indicator.
  • One controller per variable. Avoid fighting loops.
