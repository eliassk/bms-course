Chapter 09

Heat generation

Where building heat comes from: combustion, condensing boilers, cascades, weather compensation, hot water, district heating, gas safety and what happens when a boiler locks out. The BMS rarely fires a burner itself, but it decides when, how hot and how many. Doing that well is most of the energy story.

9.1 Heat and combustion basics

Heating is an energy balance. A building loses heat through walls, windows and ventilation. The plant must put the same power back in. Three quantities show up everywhere:

QuantityUnitTypical numbers
Power (heat rate)kWFlat 5–10 kW, school 300 kW, hospital several MW
EnergykWh, MWh, GJ1 m³ natural gas ≈ 10–11 kWh (gross); 1 GJ = 277.8 kWh
Temperature°C, K for differencesRadiators 55/45 °C, floor heating 35/28 °C

Water carries the heat. The key equation of all hydronic work is

Q [kW] = ṁ [kg/s] × 4.19 [kJ/kg·K] × ΔT [K]

So 100 kW at a 20 K difference needs about 1.19 kg/s, roughly 4.3 m³/h. Halve ΔT and you must double the flow. The pump power for that grows much faster than linearly. Remember this; it returns in condensing boilers and district heating.

What burns

Natural gas is mostly methane: CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O + heat. Air is about 21 % oxygen, so one m³ of methane needs roughly 9.5 m³ of air in theory. Real burners add more, because a perfect mix does not exist. The ratio of actual to theoretical air is lambda (λ). λ = 1.3 means 30 % excess air.

  • Too little air (λ near or below 1): incomplete combustion, carbon monoxide (CO), soot, unstable flame.
  • Too much air: the extra air is heated and blown up the flue for nothing. Flue losses rise, the dew point drops, condensing gets harder.

A service engineer measures the flue gas with an analyser: O₂ or CO₂, CO, flue temperature. From these the analyser computes λ and the flue loss. Modern premix boilers aim for about 8.5–9.5 % CO₂ (λ ≈ 1.2–1.35) on natural gas, but always check the manufacturer's table: it differs per gas family and per load.

Try it: excess air

Gross vs net calorific value

Burning methane makes water vapour. If the vapour leaves up the flue, its latent heat is lost. The net (lower, NCV, Hi) value assumes it is lost; the gross (higher, GCV, Hs) value includes it. For natural gas, GCV is about 11 % higher than NCV. European boiler data has long been quoted against NCV, which is why condensing boilers can claim "108 % efficiency". Against GCV the same boiler is perhaps 97 %. Nothing breaks physics; the reference just moved.

When comparing a gas bill (usually in gross kWh) with boiler output, mixing NCV and GCV gives an apparent 10 % error. Always check which basis a datasheet, meter or bill uses.

A burner runs at λ = 1.8. What is the main effect?

  • Q = ṁ · c · ΔT. Lower ΔT means more flow for the same power.
  • λ is the excess air ratio. Too low makes CO, too high wastes heat.
  • Know whether efficiency is on net or gross calorific value.

9.2 Condensing gas boilers

A condensing boiler has a large, corrosion-resistant heat exchanger (stainless steel or aluminium-silicon) that cools the flue gas below its dew point. The water vapour condenses on the exchanger and gives up its latent heat. For natural gas at typical excess air the dew point is around 55–57 °C. The flue gas can only get as cold as the water it meets, so the deciding factor is the return water temperature.

Try it: return temperature vs efficiency

Read the curve from right to left. Above the dew point the boiler is a good non-condensing boiler, about 96–97 % on net value. As the return drops below roughly 55 °C, condensation starts and efficiency climbs steeply. At 30 °C return the boiler may reach 106–108 % net.

What the BMS controls that affects this

LeverEffect on return temperature
Weather-compensated flow (9.4)Lower flow temperature in mild weather means lower return. The biggest single win.
Hydraulic separation (low-loss header)If the boiler pump moves more water than the system takes, hot flow short-circuits back into the return. Return rises.
Three-port valves and bypassesBypasses send hot water straight back. Prefer two-port valves with variable-speed pumps.
Balancing and TRVsUnbalanced radiators pass too much water and return it hot.
DHW priorityTank reheats need 70–80 °C flow and push return up. Keep them short.

Modulation and turndown

A modulating premix burner varies its power with a fan speed and a gas valve that follows the air. Turndown ratio is maximum over minimum power. 1:5 is common, 1:10 good, some boilers claim 1:20. Below the minimum the burner must cycle on and off. Short cycling wastes energy (each start needs a pre-purge that cools the exchanger), wears the igniter and increases emissions. A boiler that starts more than a few times an hour at mild loads is a commissioning problem.

Most boilers accept a demand from the BMS in one of four ways: an on/off contact (the boiler uses its own internal curve), a 0–10 V signal (flow setpoint or power), a bus (OpenTherm, Modbus, BACnet, or a vendor bus through a gateway), or through the manufacturer's cascade manager.

Condensate

A 100 kW boiler in condensing mode can make several litres of condensate per hour. It is mildly acidic (pH around 3–5). Codes often require a neutraliser on larger plant. A frozen or blocked condensate drain is a classic winter fault: the siphon fills, the boiler detects it and locks out.

A school replaced old cast-iron boilers with condensing ones but kept the 80/60 °C constant-temperature circuits and three-port mixing valves. Return water stayed at 60 °C+ and the "condensing" boilers never condensed. Adding weather compensation and converting the bypasses brought the return down to 35–45 °C most of the season and cut gas use by roughly 10 %.

A condensing boiler reports 62 °C return water all winter. What is the likely conclusion?

  • Condensing depends on return temperature, below about 55 °C.
  • The BMS lowers return via weather compensation and good hydraulics.
  • Watch turndown and cycling: starts per hour is a key metric.

9.3 Cascades and lead/lag

Large plants use several boilers instead of one big one. Reasons: redundancy (one fails, others carry the load), wide turndown (four 100 kW boilers at 1:5 give 20–400 kW), and maintenance without a shutdown. A cascade controller decides how many boilers run and at what power.

Staging logic

  1. The controller compares the common flow temperature (after the header) with its setpoint.
  2. A PI loop produces a total power demand, 0–100 % of plant capacity.
  3. The lead boiler starts first. When it has run near full power for a delay (e.g. 5–10 min) and the flow is still below setpoint, the next lag boiler starts.
  4. When demand drops, the last boiler to start stops first, after its own delay.

Two strategies split the power:

StrategyHowWhen it fits
Series / sequentialFill each boiler before starting the next.Non-condensing boilers, where high load per unit is efficient.
Parallel / unisonStart more boilers early and run them all at low modulation.Condensing boilers. They are most efficient at low fire because the exchanger is oversized for the flow.

To even out wear, the lead role rotates: every week, every N hours of runtime, or on the boiler with the fewest hours. Boilers that fail or lock out are skipped and the next one is promoted.

Try it: cascade simulator

Hydraulics of a cascade

Each boiler usually has its own pump and an isolating or motorised valve, so that water does not flow through idle boilers. That would cool the common flow and also waste heat through the idle boiler's casing. A low-loss header or plate heat exchanger separates the boiler circuit from the system circuits.

If idle boilers keep their pumps running, cold water mixes into the header. The controller sees low flow temperature and starts more boilers, which makes it worse. Check that pumps and valves follow the burner state.

For a cascade of condensing boilers, which strategy is usually more efficient?

  • Cascades give redundancy and wide turndown.
  • Rotate the lead boiler by time or runtime; skip faulted units.
  • Idle boilers MUST be isolated hydraulically.

9.4 Weather compensation and heating curves

Heat loss is roughly proportional to the difference between indoor and outdoor temperature. A radiator's output depends on how much hotter it is than the room. So the colder it is outside, the hotter the water must be. A heating curve (weather compensation curve) maps outdoor temperature to flow temperature setpoint. It is open-loop: it does not measure the room, it predicts the need.

Try it: heating curve

Tuning the curve

  • Too cold only in cold weather, fine when mild: slope too low. Raise the slope.
  • Too cold (or warm) at all outdoor temperatures: shift the curve in parallel.
  • Change one thing at a time and wait at least a day. Buildings, and floor heating especially, are slow.

Refinements found in controllers:

FeatureWhat it does
Outdoor temperature filteringUses a damped value (hours) so a sunny afternoon does not drop the flow instantly. Heavy buildings need more damping.
Room influenceA reference room sensor corrects the curve. Turns open-loop into partly closed-loop.
Summer cut-offStops heating above an outdoor limit, e.g. 16–18 °C.
Optimum startLearns how long the building takes to warm up and starts just in time before occupancy.
Boost and setbackRaises the curve after night setback, lowers it in unoccupied times.
Max/min limitsFloor heating MUST have a hard flow limit (often 45–55 °C), backed by a safety thermostat.
An office has radiators on one circuit and floor heating on another, both from one boiler. The boiler follows the highest demand (the radiator curve, e.g. 65 °C at −15 °C). The floor circuit uses a mixing valve with its own lower curve (e.g. 38 °C at −15 °C). The BMS sends the boiler the maximum of all circuit demands plus a few kelvin margin.
The outdoor sensor belongs on a north-facing wall, out of the sun and away from exhaust air, windows and flues. A sensor in the sun can push the curve down by 10 K and leave the building cold at noon.

The building is fine at +5 °C outside but too cold at −10 °C. What do you change?

  • The heating curve predicts flow temperature from outdoor temperature.
  • Slope fixes errors that grow with cold; parallel shift fixes constant errors.
  • Lower flow temperatures mean lower returns and more condensing.

9.5 Domestic hot water and legionella

Domestic hot water (DHW) is hot water for taps and showers. It is drinking water, so hygiene rules apply. Common arrangements:

TypeHowNotes
Indirect storage cylinderBoiler water flows through a coil in a tank.Most common. Needs a tank sensor and a valve or pump for "DHW priority".
Instantaneous / fresh-water stationPlate heat exchanger heats water as it flows.Little stored water, low legionella risk. Needs high peak power or a buffer tank.
Direct-fired / electricBurner or element in the tank.Simple, own controls. BMS often just monitors.

Why legionella matters

Legionella bacteria grow in water at about 20–45 °C, best around 35–37 °C. They cause Legionnaires' disease when contaminated water is inhaled as aerosol, for example in a shower. Heat kills them. The hotter, the faster: at 50 °C it takes hours, at 60 °C a few minutes, at 70 °C seconds (figures vary between studies). Typical guidance, for example in many European codes:

  • Store hot water at 60 °C or above.
  • Return from the circulation loop at 55 °C or above (some codes accept 50 °C).
  • Keep cold water below 20–25 °C.
  • Run a thermal disinfection cycle where stored temperature is lower, e.g. 70 °C for a defined period through the whole system, often weekly. The exact rules are national (e.g. DVGW W 551 in Germany, HSG274/L8 in the UK). Check your local code.

The stratified tank is the problem. The top may read 60 °C while the bottom, where cold water enters, sits at 30 °C. A destratification pump or a sensor at the bottom helps prove the whole volume was heated.

Try it: a week of tank temperature

What the BMS does for DHW

  • Schedules the reheat and the disinfection cycle (often at night when no one showers: 70 °C water scalds).
  • Proves the cycle: logs tank top, bottom and circulation return temperatures, raises an alarm if the target was not held for the required time.
  • Gives DHW priority: heating circuits pause briefly while the tank reheats.
  • Monitors the circulation pump and flags returns below 55 °C.
Thermal disinfection reaches tap temperatures that scald in seconds. Thermostatic mixing valves at outlets limit the temperature delivered to users. Disinfection of the outlets themselves usually needs a controlled, supervised procedure.
A hotel's legionella log showed the weekly 70 °C cycle "completed" every Sunday. The sensor was at the top of the tank. A second sensor at the bottom showed it never passed 52 °C because the cycle ended after 20 minutes. The fix: a destratification pump and a rule "cycle ends only when the bottom sensor has held 65 °C+ for the set time".

Which tank strategy carries the highest legionella risk?

  • Legionella grows at 20–45 °C. Store at 60 °C, circulate at 55 °C+.
  • Measure the tank bottom, not only the top.
  • The BMS schedules, logs and proves disinfection cycles.

9.6 District heating substations

In district heating (DH) the heat is made centrally, at a CHP plant, waste incinerator, large heat pump or boiler station, and piped to buildings. Each building has a substation. Most are indirect: a plate heat exchanger separates the network (primary) from the building (secondary). The building never sees network water or network pressure.

Explore the substation

Return temperature is money

Utilities care most about a low primary return. A low return raises network capacity (same pipe, larger ΔT), cuts heat losses and lets heat pumps and flue-gas condensers at the plant work better. Many tariffs include a penalty or bonus based on return temperature or "cooling" (ΔT).

A residential block received a monthly penalty for a 58 °C primary return. Trend data showed a DHW circulation loop bypassing through a stuck-open valve and a heating curve set 10 K too high. After fixing both, the return dropped to about 42 °C and the penalty became a small bonus.

Why does a DH utility reward low primary return temperatures?

  • Indirect substations separate network and building with a plate exchanger.
  • DP controller + control valve + heat meter are the core primary parts.
  • Low primary return is the main performance indicator.

9.7 Gas safety and detection

Gas safety is a certified, hard-wired layer. The BMS watches it but never replaces it. Typical elements of a gas-fired plant room:

DeviceJob
Burner control / flame safeguardSupervises ignition and flame (ionisation or UV), closes gas valves on flame loss. Certified to EN 298 or similar.
Double gas valvesTwo safety shut-off valves in series on each burner, often with valve proving (leak test) before each start.
Main gas solenoid / safety shut-off valveNormally closed, at the plant room entry. Closes on gas alarm, fire alarm or emergency stop.
Gas detectorsCatalytic or infrared sensors for methane (mounted high, it is lighter than air) or propane/LPG (low, it is heavier).
CO detectorsElectrochemical. Detect poor combustion or flue leaks.
Emergency stop buttonsAt the plant room exit. Cut the boiler supply and close the gas valve.
Ventilation interlockWhere combustion air is fan-assisted: no fan proven, no burner.

LEL and alarm stages

Flammable gas is measured as a percentage of its lower explosive limit (LEL). Methane's LEL is about 4.4–5 % by volume (sources differ: 4.4 % in IEC 60079-20-1, 5 % in older tables). 100 % LEL is a mix that can ignite. Detectors alarm far below that. Thresholds are set per project and code, but a common pattern is 10 % LEL pre-alarm and 20 % LEL main alarm with shut-off.

Sort it: who owns the function?

Close the gas valves when the flame is lost
Send a message to the on-call engineer on gas alarm
Close the main gas valve at 20 % LEL
Trend the detector reading over months to spot drift
Close the gas on a fire alarm
Remind maintenance that detectors are due for calibration
Gas detector sensors drift and age. Catalytic sensors can be poisoned by silicones. Most manufacturers require a bump test or calibration at least every 6–12 months and replacement every few years. An uncalibrated detector is a false sense of security.

Where do you mount a natural gas (methane) detector?

  • Flame safeguard, gas valves and detection are certified and hard-wired.
  • Alarms are in % LEL; typical stages 10 % and 20 %.
  • The BMS monitors, notifies and trends. It does not shut off gas.

9.8 Boiler faults, lockouts, servicing

Every burner start follows a fixed sequence run by the burner control. Understanding it explains most fault codes.

StepWhat happensTypical time
StandbyWaiting for heat demand. Safety chain checked: limit thermostat, pressure, condensate, air pressure switch.–
Pre-purgeFan blows air through the combustion chamber to clear any gas.a few s to 30 s+
IgnitionSpark on, gas valves open.–
Safety timeFlame must be detected within this window or gas closes.often 3–5 s
RunFlame proven, burner modulates.–
Post-purgeFan clears the chamber after the burner stops.a few s

Blocking vs locking

Faults come in two kinds, and the difference matters for the BMS:

  • Blocking (non-volatile... no: self-resetting) faults: a condition such as low water pressure or a high flue temperature stops the burner. When the condition clears, the boiler restarts on its own.
  • Lockout faults: something unsafe happened, typically no flame after the allowed ignition attempts, or a safety limit thermostat tripped. The burner control stays off until someone presses reset. The lockout state survives a power cut on most modern controls.

Try it: the burner sequence

Remote reset of a lockout is a sensitive feature. A lockout means the control saw something unsafe. Some boilers and codes limit the number of resets in a period, and many operators allow remote reset only once, then require a site visit. Never automate repeated resets in BMS logic.

What the BMS should read

PointWhy
Common fault / lockout (volt-free contact)Works on every boiler. The minimum.
Fault code (via bus)Tells you whether to send a technician or just top up pressure.
Flow, return, flue temperaturesEfficiency and early warnings (rising flue temperature = dirty exchanger).
System water pressureSlow loss means a leak; 1–2 bar is typical in small sealed systems.
Burner starts and hoursShort cycling and service intervals.

Servicing

An annual service is typical and often a legal or warranty requirement. It covers flue gas analysis and burner adjustment, cleaning the heat exchanger and condensate trap, checking electrodes, testing safety devices and the expansion vessel pre-charge. BMS trends make the visit faster: the engineer sees starts per day, the flue temperature trend and the fault history before opening the casing.

A cascade's lead boiler showed 40 starts per hour at mild weather. The trend explained it: minimum output 25 kW, building load in October about 15 kW. Changing the cascade to allow a longer off-time, adding a buffer volume and lowering the heating curve brought starts below 6 per hour.

A boiler stops on low water pressure and restarts by itself after the system is topped up. This is…

  • Start sequence: purge, ignite, prove flame within the safety time, run.
  • Blocking faults clear themselves; lockouts need a reset.
  • Read fault codes, starts and flue temperature, not just a common alarm.
