Chapter 14

Professional audio and AV over IP

Venues, offices and hotels now carry sound, video and control on the same Ethernet as the BMS. This chapter covers levels and gain staging, DSP platforms, Dante, AES67 and ST 2110, network audio players, display control, video distribution and the control systems that tie it all together.

14.1 Audio basics: levels, gain staging, DSP

Audio engineers talk in decibels. A decibel is a ratio on a log scale. For voltages, dB = 20 · log10(V / Vref). Double the voltage is +6 dB. Ten times the voltage is +20 dB. The unit only means something once you know the reference, which is what the suffix tells you.

Gain staging

Every stage in a chain has a noise floor at the bottom and a clip point at the top. The space between is its dynamic range. Gain staging means setting each stage so the signal sits comfortably in the middle of that window. Too low, and later gain lifts the noise with it. Too high, and the peaks flatten into harsh distortion that no later stage can undo.

At the analog-to-digital boundary you need an alignment level: which dBu equals which dBFS. Two common conventions are EBU R68 (0 dBu = −18 dBFS, so full scale is +18 dBu) and SMPTE RP 155 (+4 dBu = −20 dBFS, so full scale is +24 dBu). The 18–20 dB gap above nominal is headroom for peaks. Speech and music have peaks 10–20 dB above their average.

A 24-bit converter has a theoretical range of about 144 dB. Real converters reach roughly 110–120 dB. That is why digital systems can run with generous headroom: −18 dBFS average still leaves 90+ dB above the noise.

Try it: stage a microphone into a DSP and amplifier

What a DSP does

A digital signal processor replaces racks of analog boxes with software blocks. Typical blocks, roughly in signal order:

  • Input gain and phantom power (+48 V for condenser mics).
  • High-pass filter, e.g. 80–100 Hz on speech mics, to remove rumble and handling noise.
  • Parametric EQ: frequency, gain, Q per band.
  • Dynamics: gate, compressor, limiter. A compressor with 4:1 ratio lets 1 dB out for each 4 dB over threshold.
  • Automatic mixer (gain sharing) for many open mics, and AEC (acoustic echo cancellation) for conferencing.
  • Matrix mixer and routing: any input to any output.
  • Crossover and delay: split bands for sub and tops, align fill speakers by distance (about 2.9 ms per metre).
  • Output limiter to protect amplifiers and speakers.

Each block adds processing latency, usually well under a millisecond. Long FIR filters and AEC add more.

Two common mistakes. First, compensating a weak preamp setting with a big fader boost: the noise comes up with the signal. Second, running every fader at +10 dB "because it is not loud enough" when the real fix is at the amplifier or the preamp. Set gain at the source, then use faders near 0 dB.
In a conference room, a ceiling mic array may arrive at −45 dBu. The DSP preamp adds about +45 dB so speech averages near 0 dBu (−24 dBFS with +24 dBu alignment). AEC, auto-mix and a gentle compressor follow. The far-end send is limited at −6 dBFS so no codec ever clips.

With the alignment +4 dBu = −20 dBFS, what analog level clips the converter?

  • dB is a ratio. The suffix (u, V, FS, SPL) gives the reference.
  • Set gain early, keep faders near 0 dB, leave 18–20 dB headroom above nominal.
  • A DSP is a chain of blocks: input, filters, dynamics, matrix, delay, limiter.

14.2 DSP platforms: BSS Soundweb London

Installed-sound DSPs are configurable, not programmable in the usual sense. You draw a signal-flow design in a PC tool, compile it, and send it to the hardware. The device then runs it standalone, with no PC needed. Common platforms include BSS Soundweb London, Biamp Tesira, QSC Q-SYS, Symetrix and Yamaha. We use BSS Soundweb London as the worked example because its control model is typical and well documented.

The Soundweb London model

  • Hardware. BLU-series units (for example BLU-100, BLU-160) with fixed or card-based analog I/O. Some models add Dante or AVB, and many support BLU link, a proprietary ring that carries a large number of audio channels between units over Cat5e.
  • Software. London Architect. You drag processing objects onto a canvas, wire them and assign them to devices.
  • Control. HiQnet addressing over Ethernet. Every parameter in the design has an address. Third-party systems (a BMS, a touch panel) can set and subscribe to parameters over TCP.
  • Presets. Saved snapshots of parameters, recalled by number. "Wedding mode" or "Conference mode" is usually a preset.

Explore a design

Addressing a parameter

Third-party control of Soundweb London uses a binary protocol over TCP, often called London Direct Inject. Each parameter is identified by a HiQnet-style address: a node (the device), a virtual device (the audio processing section), an object (one block in the design) and a parameter (one control in that block). London Architect shows these addresses for each control.

A message has a start byte, a command type, the address, a value and a checksum, then an end byte. Bytes that collide with the framing characters are escaped. The main commands are set, subscribe (the device then pushes changes), set percent and recall preset. Subscribing is what keeps a panel in sync when someone else changes the volume.

Object IDs can change when the design is edited and recompiled. Treat the address list as part of the design's release: export it, version it, and re-check integrations after every DSP file change.
A bar and restaurant has six zones. A BMS schedule recalls preset 2 ("evening") at 18:00, which raises background music by 4 dB and enables the terrace zone. A wall panel per zone sets volume within −40…−10 dB. The integration subscribes to every zone gain, so the BMS dashboard always shows the true level.

A BMS sets a zone volume, and staff also change it on a wall controller. How does the BMS keep showing the right value?

  • DSP designs are drawn in a PC tool, compiled, and run standalone.
  • Every controllable parameter has an address: node, virtual device, object, parameter.
  • Subscribe for feedback. Version the address list with the design.

14.3 Dante

Dante, from Audinate, is the most widely deployed audio-over-IP system. Thousands of products from hundreds of manufacturers ship with it. It carries uncompressed audio as UDP over ordinary IP networks, with sample-accurate sync and latency settable down to a fraction of a millisecond.

How it works

  • Clocking. Devices elect one leader clock using PTP (IEEE 1588). Classic Dante uses PTPv1. In AES67 mode a device also speaks PTPv2. Each device's media clock locks to the leader.
  • Discovery. Devices announce themselves with mDNS/DNS-SD. Dante Controller, the free routing tool, shows them and lets you route by device and channel name.
  • Flows. Audio travels in flows. A unicast flow goes to one receiver and by default carries up to 4 channels. A multicast flow goes to many receivers and needs IGMP snooping on the switches.
  • Latency. The receiver plays each sample at a fixed time after it was captured, set by the latency value: for example 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2 or 5 ms. All receivers with the same setting are sample-aligned.
  • Redundancy. Many devices have a secondary port. Primary and secondary run on separate networks, and each packet is sent on both.

The classic Dante fault in building networks: a switch with EEE enabled, or an IT-managed core that re-marks DSCP to zero. Clock sync wobbles, Dante Controller shows clock errors, and audio clicks every few seconds. Check the switch configuration first, not the audio devices.
A conference centre uses Dante between a stage box, two DSPs and twelve amplifier channels, on its own VLAN across three floors. Latency is set to 1 ms everywhere. The DSP is set as preferred leader, so the clock does not move when the stage box is unplugged between events.

Why do all receivers set to 1 ms latency play the same sample at the same moment?

  • Dante = uncompressed audio over UDP/IP, PTP clocking, mDNS discovery, routing by name.
  • Network rules: gigabit, DSCP QoS with PTP first, EEE off, IGMP snooping for multicast.
  • Latency is a fixed playout delay. Choose the lowest value that is reliable on your network.

14.4 AES67 and SMPTE ST 2110

Dante is a product. Several other audio-over-IP systems exist: Ravenna, Livewire+, Q-LAN and others. They do not speak each other's native protocols. AES67 (first published 2013) is the common denominator they all agreed on, so streams can cross between them.

What AES67 specifies

LayerAES67 choice
ClockPTPv2 (IEEE 1588-2008), default or media profile
TransportRTP over UDP over IP, usually multicast
EncodingLinear PCM, L16 or L24, 48 kHz required
Packet time1 ms required (48 samples); others optional
Channels1–8 per stream at 1 ms
DescriptionSDP describes the stream
QoSDSCP marking recommended for clock and media
DiscoveryNot mandated. SAP, mDNS/RTSP, SIP or manual SDP are all used.

The missing discovery piece is why "AES67 compatible" does not always mean "plug and play". You often have to enable AES67 mode on each device, pick a multicast address, and paste or announce the SDP yourself.

PTP in four timestamps

PTP measures the offset between a follower and the leader with four timestamps. The leader sends Sync at t1, the follower receives it at t2. The follower sends Delay_Req at t3, the leader receives it at t4. If the path is symmetric:

delay  = ((t2 − t1) + (t4 − t3)) / 2
offset = ((t2 − t1) − (t4 − t3)) / 2

Switches that queue packets break the symmetry. Boundary clocks and transparent clocks in PTP-aware switches remove that error, which matters most for video.

SMPTE ST 2110

Broadcast took the same idea further. ST 2110 sends video, audio and metadata as separate RTP streams, all timed by PTP. A receiver re-joins them by timestamp. This lets you route audio without touching video.

PartContent
ST 2110-10System timing (PTP via SMPTE ST 2059)
ST 2110-20Uncompressed video
ST 2110-21Traffic shaping of video senders
ST 2110-22Compressed video, e.g. JPEG XS
ST 2110-30Audio, based on AES67
ST 2110-31AES3 transparent audio
ST 2110-40Ancillary data (captions, timecode)

Discovery and connection in ST 2110 plants are usually handled by AMWA NMOS: IS-04 for registration and discovery, IS-05 for connection management. Buildings rarely need ST 2110 itself, but a venue with a broadcast truck dock or a TV studio may, and the AES67 audio layer is what connects to the house system.

Dante's native clock is PTPv1 and AES67 uses PTPv2. A mixed network has two PTP domains. Make sure one device bridges them deliberately, and that no second grandmaster appears by accident on the AES67 side.
A radio station sends its studio console output as an AES67 stream into a building's Dante network so the lobby and café can play the live programme. One Dante device has AES67 mode enabled and subscribes to the console's multicast stream, using the SDP the console announces with SAP.

Which piece does AES67 leave open, causing most interoperability headaches?

  • AES67 = PTPv2 + RTP + L24/48 kHz + 1 ms packets + SDP. Discovery is not included.
  • PTP computes offset from four timestamps and assumes a symmetric path.
  • ST 2110 splits video, audio and data into PTP-timed streams; its audio is AES67.

14.5 Network audio clients: Squeezelite, LMS

Background music does not need sample-accurate sync to a mixing console. It needs to be cheap, reliable, schedulable and controllable from a BMS. A popular open-source answer is the Lyrion Music Server (LMS, formerly Logitech Media Server, originally SlimServer) with Squeezelite players.

Architecture

  • Server. LMS holds the library, playlists, radio stations and streaming plugins. It decodes or transcodes as needed and sends the stream to each player. It runs on a NAS, a VM, a container or a Raspberry Pi.
  • Player. Squeezelite is a small headless client for Linux, Windows and macOS. It plays to any local audio device: a USB interface, a sound card, an ASIO driver, or a virtual Dante soundcard.
  • Protocols. Players talk to the server with SlimProto on TCP port 3483 and fetch audio over HTTP (commonly port 9000). Discovery uses UDP 3483. Control from outside uses the CLI on TCP 9090 or JSON-RPC over HTTP at /jsonrpc.js.
  • Sync groups. The server can group players so they play the same stream in step. Sync is good enough for adjacent rooms (a few milliseconds), not for a stereo pair split across two devices.

A typical player command line

squeezelite -n "Cafe" -m 02:00:00:00:00:01 -o hw:CARD=USB -a 200 -s 192.168.20.10
OptionMeaning
-nPlayer name shown in LMS
-mMAC address used as the player ID. LMS stores settings, sync groups and alarms per ID.
-oOutput device (list them with -l)
-aOutput buffer settings (ALSA: buffer time in ms)
-sServer address. Without it the player discovers the server by broadcast.

Controlling it from a BMS

The JSON-RPC interface takes a player ID and a command array. These are the same commands as the CLI:

POST http://lms:9000/jsonrpc.js
{"id":1,"method":"slim.request",
 "params":["02:00:00:00:00:01",["mixer","volume","35"]]}

["power","1"]            switch the player on
["playlist","play","http://stream.example/radio.mp3"]
["sync","02:00:00:00:00:02"]   join another player's group
["status","-","1"]       read current state

For push updates, the CLI on port 9090 supports subscribe and streams events as text lines. Many home and building platforms ship an LMS integration that wraps this.

Pin the player MAC with -m. If you let Squeezelite pick it from the network card, a replaced NIC, a new VM or a Docker host change gives the player a new identity. LMS then sees a brand-new player, and every schedule, group and BMS reference to the old one silently stops working.
A hotel lobby, café and terrace each have a small player box feeding a DSP input. LMS runs in a container. At 07:00 the BMS powers the three players, syncs them, starts a radio stream and sets volume 30. At 23:00 it stops the café and terrace and keeps the lobby on at 20.

After a server rebuild, LMS shows a new "Cafe" player and the old schedules no longer run. Most likely cause?

  • LMS serves and syncs; Squeezelite plays to any local audio output.
  • SlimProto on TCP 3483, HTTP on 9000, CLI on 9090, JSON-RPC at /jsonrpc.js.
  • The MAC is the player identity. Pin it.

14.6 Displays and TV control (RS-232, IP, CEC)

A BMS or AV system often needs to switch displays on at opening time, select inputs and switch them off at night. There are four common ways, and they differ a lot in reliability.

Command examples

Why IP power-on fails

In standby, many TVs turn their network interface off to meet energy rules. A TCP "power on" then has nobody to talk to. Fixes, in order of preference: use RS-232; enable the display's "network standby" or "power on by network" setting (this costs a watt or two); or send Wake-on-LAN, which works on some models only. Commercial "signage" displays are far more predictable than consumer TVs here.

Projectors and PJLink

PJLink is a JBMIA standard for projectors and some displays. It is plain ASCII over TCP port 4352: %1POWR 1 turns on, %1POWR ? asks state, %1INPT 31 selects an input. Class 2 adds more commands and notifications. Projectors also report lamp hours and errors, which are worth trending in the BMS.

HDMI-CEC

CEC is a slow single-wire bus inside HDMI (pin 13), at a few hundred bits per second. Devices have logical addresses (0 = TV, 4 = playback device, 15 = broadcast) and physical addresses from the HDMI topology. It is great for a media player that wakes the TV when it starts playing. It is unreliable through matrix switchers, extenders and AV receivers that do not pass or re-generate CEC correctly.

Every display reports "on" too early. After power-on, most ignore input commands for 5–15 seconds while booting. Sequence your commands: power on, poll until the reply says on, then select the input. A control system that fires both at once works on the bench and fails in the field.
A museum has 18 commercial displays. Each is daisy-chained on RS-232 out/in, so a single serial port with IDs 1–18 controls all of them. The BMS polls power state every minute and raises an alarm when a display is off during opening hours, which catches failed panels and visitors who press the power button.

A consumer TV ignores IP "power on" but obeys IP "power off". What is happening?

  • Prefer RS-232, then IP with network standby, then CEC. IR only as last resort.
  • Always read state back. Wait for boot before sending input commands.
  • PJLink (TCP 4352) is the common projector protocol.

14.7 Video distribution and signage

Moving video around a building used to mean HDMI cables and matrix switchers. Today it is either point-to-point extension over twisted pair (HDBaseT) or AV over IP: an encoder at each source, a decoder at each display, and an Ethernet switch as the matrix.

The numbers that drive everything

Uncompressed video bit rate is width × height × frames per second × bits per pixel. 1080p60 with 8-bit 4:2:2 is about 2 Gb/s of active picture. 4K60 with 8-bit 4:4:4 is about 12 Gb/s. A gigabit network cannot carry either without compression, which is the central trade-off of AV over IP.

Network design for AV over IP

  • Every decoder joins a multicast group. Without IGMP snooping and a querier, one 800 Mb/s stream floods every port and the network falls over. This is the number one AV-over-IP failure.
  • Use a dedicated VLAN and non-blocking switches. Check the uplink: 8 × 900 Mb/s streams need a 10 Gb uplink.
  • Jumbo frames are sometimes required by the vendor. Enable them consistently, or not at all.
  • HDCP content protection still applies. Encoders and decoders negotiate it; non-HDCP displays go black.

Digital signage

Signage is content scheduling, not video transport. A CMS holds playlists and schedules. A player next to each screen (a dedicated device such as BrightSign, a system-on-chip inside a commercial display, or a small PC running something like Xibo) downloads content in advance and plays it locally. If the network drops, playback continues from cache. The BMS link is usually: power the displays on a schedule, read their health, and trigger an emergency or event layout from an external input.

Signage displays run 12–24 hours a day. Consumer TVs are not rated for that, and static logos can cause image retention. Use displays rated for the duty cycle, and enable pixel shift or screen savers where supported.
A sports bar sends four sources (two sat boxes, a streaming box, a laptop input) to 14 screens over 1G AV over IP with light compression. Each stream is about 800 Mb/s, so the encoders and decoders sit on a 48-port switch with IGMP snooping, and the switch has a 10 Gb uplink to the second floor's switch.

After adding AV-over-IP decoders, office PCs on the same switch slow to a crawl. First thing to check?

  • Bit rate = pixels × fps × bits per pixel. 4K60 uncompressed is ~12 Gb/s.
  • Pick compression by latency need: 10G light, 1G mezzanine, H.264 for distance.
  • AV over IP is multicast: IGMP snooping, querier, VLAN, enough uplink.

14.8 Control systems: Crestron, Q-SYS, Extron

An AV control system is to AV what a DDC controller is to HVAC: a processor that talks to every device in its native protocol and offers one simple interface to people. A touch panel button "Presentation" might power the projector, lower the screen, select the laptop input, unmute the mics, dim the lights to 30 % and close the blinds.

Where AV control meets the BMS

Draw the boundary on purpose. The AV system owns room scenes and device sequencing. The BMS owns building-wide schedules, energy and alarms. They exchange a small set of well-defined signals:

Power projector, wait, select HDMI 2
Opening hours calendar for the whole building
Room occupied / in use
Mute all microphones in room 2
Fire alarm active → stop programme audio
Notify a technician that a projector lamp failed
Projector lamp hours and error state
Never let two controllers drive the same device independently. If the AV system and the BMS both send power commands to a display, they will fight: one turns it on for a meeting, the other turns it off on schedule. Pick one owner per device and let the other side request through it.
A university lecture hall runs a Q-SYS Core for audio and control. The campus BMS reads "room in use" over the Core's external control interface to drive ventilation, and at 22:00 sends "system off". The Core then runs its own shutdown sequence: mute, projector off, wait for cool-down, amplifiers to standby.

The BMS should switch a lecture hall's AV off at night. What is the best design?

  • A control processor turns intents into device sequences and shows real feedback.
  • Crestron, Q-SYS and Extron differ in programming model and external API.
  • One owner per device. Exchange a small, documented interface with the BMS.
