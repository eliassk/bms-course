Chapter 17

Electrical power and UPS

Every controller, switch and server in a building runs on electricity that someone else supplies, and that supply fails, sags and gets dirty. This chapter covers the low-voltage side a BMS engineer must understand: distribution boards and protection, UPS types and sizing, monitoring UPSs over the network, shutting systems down in the right order, generators, solar with batteries, and power quality.

17.1 Distribution boards and protection

Power enters a building at the service entrance: the utility cable, a main fuse or breaker, and the revenue meter. In Europe a typical supply is 3-phase 230/400 V at 50 Hz. In North America it is 120/240 V split phase for houses and 120/208 V or 277/480 V 3-phase for commercial buildings, at 60 Hz. From the main distribution board (MDB, or main switchboard) cables run to sub-distribution boards on each floor or for each plant room. Each board feeds final circuits: sockets, lighting, an air handling unit, a server rack.

This tree is drawn as a single-line diagram (SLD). One line stands for all phases plus neutral and earth. The SLD is the first document to ask for when you plan BMS power: it tells you which board feeds what, which protective device sits where, and which circuits are on UPS or generator.

MCB trip curves set how much inrush current is tolerated before the magnetic trip acts: curve B at 3–5 × In, curve C at 5–10 × In, curve D at 10–20 × In. A UPS, a transformer or a large switch-mode supply has a big inrush when switched on. A B16 feeding a 3 kVA UPS may trip at power-up where a C16 would not.

RCD types matter for electronics. Type AC detects only sinusoidal leakage. Type A adds pulsating DC. Type F adds mixed frequencies, typical of single-phase variable speed drives. Type B also detects smooth DC leakage, which 3-phase VFDs, EV chargers and some PV inverters can produce. A smooth DC leakage can blind an upstream type A device. Follow the equipment manual and local rules on which type is required.

Selectivity (discrimination) means that only the device closest to the fault trips. A short in one socket circuit should open its B16, not the 63 A feeder to the whole floor. Selectivity is designed with the manufacturers' tables. Also mind leakage: many PCs and PSUs on one 30 mA RCD add up their EMC filter leakage. As a rule of thumb, keep the standing leakage under about a third of the rated residual current.

What the BMS reads from a board

Most breakers can have an auxiliary contact (on/off) and a fault/trip contact. Wired to digital inputs they tell the BMS "the AHU breaker has tripped", which is far better than inferring it from a missing temperature. Modern boards add energy meters on Modbus or M-Bus (Chapter 18), and some breakers report current, temperature and trip cause directly over Modbus or a wireless gateway. A Type 2 SPD usually has a remote signalling contact that says when its cartridge is worn out.

A 6-storey office has a 400 A MDB, one 63 A sub-board per floor and a 125 A mechanical board in the basement. The BMS reads trip contacts from 14 breakers that feed critical plant, the SPD status contact, and three Modbus meters. The IT/BMS board is fed from its own breaker on the MDB, so a tripped floor RCD never takes down the network core.
Never put BMS controllers, network switches and the fire alarm interface on a socket circuit shared with kettles and cleaners' vacuums. A single tripped RCD then takes out building control. Give control panels dedicated, labelled circuits, ideally behind a UPS.

A 3-phase VFD feeds a fan. Which RCD type is generally required to detect its possible smooth DC leakage?

  • The single-line diagram is the map: get it before you plan BMS power.
  • MCBs protect cables; RCDs protect people; SPDs protect equipment from surges.
  • Curve and RCD type must match the load (inrush, DC leakage).
  • Wire trip and SPD contacts into the BMS; give control panels dedicated circuits.

17.2 UPS topologies and sizing

An uninterruptible power supply stores energy in a battery and delivers it when the mains fails. It bridges short outages and gives systems time to shut down cleanly or for a generator to start. UPSs differ in how the load is connected in normal operation. IEC 62040-3 classifies them by what the output depends on: VFD (voltage and frequency dependent on input), VI (voltage independent, frequency dependent) and VFI (voltage and frequency independent).

Online units also have a static bypass: if the inverter fails or is overloaded, an electronic switch connects the load to mains without a break. A separate maintenance bypass switch lets you remove the UPS for service while the load stays powered.

Sizing: VA, W and runtime

UPSs are rated in VA (apparent power) and W (real power). Modern units have an output power factor of 0.9 or 1.0, so a 3000 VA unit delivers 2700 or 3000 W. Size on watts. Add up the measured or nameplate input power of each device, add 20–30 % headroom, and stay under about 80 % load so there is room for growth and inrush.

Runtime depends on the battery energy and the load. A rough first estimate: runtime ≈ battery Wh × inverter efficiency ÷ load W. Real runtime is shorter at high load, because a lead-acid battery delivers fewer usable Wh when discharged fast (the Peukert effect). Manufacturers publish runtime tables; use those for the final number.

Try it: runtime calculator

Batteries

Most UPSs use VRLA (valve regulated lead-acid, AGM) batteries. Their design life is often quoted as 3–5 years for standard blocks and up to 10–12 years for long-life types, but heat shortens it dramatically: a common rule of thumb is half the life for every 8–10 K above 20–25 °C. Lithium-ion (often LFP) UPS batteries cost more but last longer, weigh less and tolerate heat better. Battery packs are added as external battery modules (EBMs) to extend runtime.

A BMS rack holds a server (180 W), two PoE switches (2 × 120 W), a firewall (30 W), and a controller power supply (60 W): 510 W. With 25 % headroom that is 640 W. A 1500 VA / 1350 W online UPS runs at about 38 %. With internal batteries of roughly 9 Ah × 4 × 12 V (≈430 Wh nominal) it gives perhaps 25–35 minutes, enough to ride through short outages and shut down cleanly on longer ones.
Runtime tables assume new batteries at 25 °C. After four summers in a hot plant room, a "30 minute" UPS may deliver 8. Schedule a self-test and a real runtime calibration at least yearly, and track battery replacement dates.

Which topology has zero transfer time because the inverter always feeds the load?

  • Offline (VFD) < line-interactive (VI) < online double conversion (VFI) in protection.
  • Size on watts with headroom; aim for under ~80 % load.
  • Runtime ≈ Wh × efficiency ÷ W, shorter at high load and with aged batteries.
  • Heat kills lead-acid batteries; test and replace on schedule.

17.3 UPS monitoring: NUT, SNMP

A UPS that nobody watches is a UPS that silently has dead batteries. Monitoring answers three questions: is the UPS on mains or battery, how long can it last, and is it healthy (battery test result, alarms, temperature). There are three common ways to get this data.

InterfaceHow it worksGood for
USB / serialUSB HID Power Device class, or a vendor protocol on RS-232. One host reads it.A single server or a small edge box next to the UPS
Network management card (SNMP, HTTP, often Modbus TCP)A card in the UPS with its own IP. Speaks SNMP (RFC 1628 UPS-MIB and vendor MIBs), web UI, email, sometimes Modbus TCP and BACnet.Racks with many servers, BMS integration, data centres
Dry contactsRelay outputs: "on battery", "low battery", "fault".Hard-wiring to a PLC or BMS digital input; simple and robust

Network UPS Tools (NUT)

NUT is the open-source standard on Linux and BSD, also used inside many NAS products and Home Assistant. It has three parts:

  • Drivers talk to the hardware: usbhid-ups for most USB UPSs, snmp-ups for network cards, nutdrv_qx for many generic units.
  • upsd is the data server. It exposes every UPS on TCP port 3493.
  • upsmon runs on each protected host. It watches the status and shuts the host down. One upsmon is the primary (formerly "master", the host with the UPS attached); others are secondary ("slave").

Clients like upsc read variables with standard names. The key one is ups.status, a set of flags: OL (on line), OB (on battery), LB (low battery), CHRG, RB (replace battery), BYPASS, OVER (overload) and FSD (forced shutdown). Standard names make NUT a neat integration layer: the BMS asks for battery.runtime without caring about the brand.

Polling every 10–30 s is enough for trends. A school has eight UPSs from two brands. Each has a network card. One small Linux VM runs NUT with eight snmp-ups drivers. The BMS reads ups.status, battery.charge and battery.runtime for all of them from upsd through one integration and raises an alarm on OB, LB or RB.
The network card and the switch between it and the monitoring host must themselves be on UPS power. Otherwise the first thing a power cut does is make the UPS invisible.

ups.status reads OB LB. What does upsmon on a typical host do?

  • Monitor via USB, a network card (SNMP/Modbus) or dry contacts.
  • NUT: drivers → upsd (port 3493) → upsmon on each host; standard variable names.
  • OB + LB means shut down; RB means replace batteries.
  • Poll and use traps; keep the monitoring path itself on UPS.

17.4 Graceful shutdown chains

When the battery is going to run out, it is better to stop systems cleanly than to let them crash. A database killed mid-write may corrupt, a hypervisor with dirty caches may lose VMs, and a storage array that loses power hard can take a long time to recover. A shutdown chain stops systems in dependency order: consumers first, then services, then storage, then the hypervisor, and the network last. On restore, the order reverses.

Triggers

  • Low battery (LB): the UPS says "about 2–5 minutes left". Simple but late. Shutting down 15 VMs may take longer than what is left.
  • Runtime threshold: start when battery.runtime drops below e.g. 600 s.
  • Time on battery: start after e.g. 5 minutes on battery, no matter how much is left. Long outages are rarely short: if the mains has not returned in 5 minutes it will probably be out for longer.

When upsmon on the primary host decides to shut down, it sets FSD on upsd. Every secondary sees FSD and shuts down. The primary waits for secondaries to disconnect (up to HOSTSYNC, 15 s by default) and then shuts itself down. Finally it can tell the UPS to switch its output off after a delay (upsdrvctl shutdown / the driver's "shutdown.return" command). That last step is important: when the mains returns, the UPS restarts its output and the servers boot again thanks to "restore on AC power" in the BIOS. Without it, a short outage that ends after the servers shut down but before the battery ran out leaves everything off.

Tools differ by platform. VMware environments often use vendor software (Eaton IPM, APC PowerChute Network Shutdown) or scripts that call the hypervisor API. Proxmox and plain Linux hosts usually run NUT upsmon with a custom SHUTDOWNCMD. Synology and QNAP NAS units include NUT and can act as the upsd server for other hosts.

A small BMS server room: one online 3 kVA UPS, a Proxmox host with 6 VMs, a NAS and two switches. The NAS has the UPS on USB and runs upsd. upsmon on the Proxmox host is a secondary with SHUTDOWNCMD that shuts down VMs, then the host. Policy: start after 5 minutes on battery. At a typical 900 W load the UPS gives 28 minutes, so the chain finishes with over 15 minutes of margin. The PLCs and field controllers run on their own 24 V DC UPS modules and keep running.
Field controllers should not be in the IT shutdown chain. They are designed to lose power and come back. What matters is that their outputs fail to a safe state, which is a design question (Chapter 08), not a UPS question.

Why should the primary tell the UPS to cut its output (with automatic return) at the end of the chain?

  • Shut down in dependency order; restart in reverse.
  • Trigger on time on battery or runtime, not only on low battery.
  • FSD propagates the decision from primary to secondaries.
  • End with "UPS off, return on power" plus BIOS "restore on AC".

17.5 Generators and transfer switches

A UPS covers minutes. A standby generator covers hours or days, limited by fuel. Diesel is most common; gas-fired units are used where piped gas is available. Typical building sets range from 20 kVA for a small site to several MVA for hospitals and data centres. A generator cannot take load instantly: it must crank, reach speed and stabilise voltage and frequency. A healthy diesel set can do this in about 10 s. Many codes for life-safety loads require restoration within 10 s (for example NFPA 110 "Type 10" in the US), but check your local regulations.

The automatic transfer switch (ATS) chooses between utility and generator. Its controller monitors utility voltage and frequency, starts the generator, waits until it is stable, and transfers. It is mechanically or electrically interlocked so both sources can never be connected together.

An open transition ATS breaks before it makes: the load sees a short gap (typically 100 ms or more) on retransfer too. A closed transition ATS briefly parallels both sources (under ~100 ms) when both are healthy and in sync, so retransfer is seamless. Closed transition usually needs utility approval.

UPS and generator must be compatible. A UPS rectifier can draw harmonic current that distorts a small generator's voltage, and some UPSs reject the generator's wandering frequency and stay on battery. Rules of thumb call for a generator 1.5–2 × the UPS rating for older rectifiers; modern IGBT rectifiers with low input THD need less. UPS settings such as a wider input frequency window and a slow "walk-in" (ramping the rectifier load over seconds) help.

What the BMS monitors

Generator controllers (Deep Sea Electronics, ComAp, Woodward and others) expose Modbus RTU/TCP with engine speed, oil pressure, coolant temperature, fuel level, battery voltage, kW, kVA, frequency and alarm lists. The ATS gives position (normal/emergency) and "source available" contacts. Key BMS alarms: generator failed to start, low fuel, not in auto, starter battery low, and ATS on emergency longer than expected.

A hotel has a 250 kVA diesel set with a 1000 l day tank (about 20 h at 60 % load, depending on the engine). The ATS feeds life-safety, lifts, kitchens' cold rooms, the IT room UPS and the heating plant. Guest-room sockets are not on the generator. The BMS logs every monthly exercise, the fuel level and the starter battery voltage, and sends an alarm if the controller is left in "manual" or "off".
The commonest generator failure is the generator not being in auto mode, followed by a flat starter battery and stale fuel. All three are cheap to monitor. Monitor them.

Why does an ATS wait 5–30 minutes before switching back to a restored utility?

  • UPS covers seconds to minutes; generator covers hours; the UPS bridges the start gap.
  • The ATS senses, starts, waits, transfers and retransfers with timers.
  • Size the generator for UPS harmonics and frequency behaviour.
  • Monitor auto mode, fuel, starter battery and ATS position.

17.6 PV, inverters, batteries

Rooftop photovoltaics (PV) turn many buildings into generators. PV modules produce DC. A module is rated in Wp (watt-peak) at standard test conditions: 1000 W/m², 25 °C cell temperature. A typical module today is 400–450 Wp. In central Europe, 1 kWp yields roughly 900–1100 kWh per year; in southern Europe 1400–1700 kWh.

The inverter converts DC to grid-synchronous AC. Each input has an MPPT (maximum power point tracker) that continuously adjusts the string voltage to the point of maximum power. Inverter types:

TypeDescriptionExamples
String inverterOne inverter for one or more strings of modules in series, e.g. 600–1000 V DCSMA Sunny Tripower, Fronius Symo, Huawei SUN2000
Microinverter / optimiserPer module conversion or MPPT; less shading loss, more partsEnphase IQ, SolarEdge optimisers
Hybrid inverterPV inverter plus battery charger in one; may provide backupFronius GEN24, Huawei with LUNA, Victron (modular)
Battery inverter (AC coupled)Separate inverter for a battery on the AC sideSMA Sunny Island, Tesla Powerwall 2

Grid rules and islanding

Grid-tied inverters must disconnect when the grid fails (anti-islanding), so line workers are not electrocuted by a building feeding a "dead" line. This surprises owners: a standard grid-tied PV system produces nothing during a power cut. Backup operation needs an inverter and a transfer device designed to form an isolated island. Grid codes (for example EN 50549 in Europe, national rules such as VDE-AR-N 4105, IEEE 1547 in the US) also require frequency and voltage ride-through and reactive power behaviour. Some utilities cap export, e.g. a zero export or 70 % limit, which the inverter enforces using a meter at the grid connection point.

Batteries and control

Stationary storage today is mostly lithium iron phosphate (LFP): often 6000+ cycles to 70–80 % capacity, safer thermal behaviour than NMC, and 90–95 % round-trip efficiency. A battery management system (BMS here means the battery's own controller, not the building's) protects cells and reports state of charge (SoC), state of health (SoH), temperature and limits.

The building BMS usually does not control power flows second by second. The inverter does that. The BMS reads values (PV power, battery SoC, grid import/export) over Modbus TCP, often via the SunSpec register model that many inverters implement, and sets higher-level targets: a charge limit, a peak shaving threshold, a time window to charge from cheap tariffs, or a request to run the heat pump when PV is in surplus (Chapter 24).

A 30-person office has 25 kWp on the roof and a 20 kWh battery. On a clear June day PV produces about 150 kWh. The building uses 110 kWh. Without a battery, self-consumption is about 55 %; with it, roughly 75 %. The BMS pre-cools the building at noon using PV surplus and reads the hybrid inverter over Modbus TCP every 5 s.
PV strings carry DC at several hundred volts whenever light falls on them, even with the inverter off. DC isolators, labelling and firefighter shutdown requirements (rapid shutdown in some jurisdictions) are not optional.

The grid fails at noon. What does a standard grid-tied PV inverter without backup function do?

  • PV gives DC; inverters with MPPT convert it to AC.
  • Grid-tied inverters disconnect in a grid failure unless built for backup.
  • Batteries shift PV surplus to the evening and shave peaks.
  • The BMS reads via Modbus/SunSpec and sets targets; the inverter controls the flows.

17.7 Power quality

Power can be present yet bad. Power quality describes how close the supply is to an ideal sine at nominal voltage and frequency. In Europe, EN 50160 describes what the public grid should deliver: for low voltage, the 10-minute mean voltage stays within ±10 % of 230 V for 95 % of a week, and total harmonic distortion (THD) of voltage stays at or below 8 %. Note these are characteristics, not a guarantee of perfection at every instant.

Harmonics in buildings

Switch-mode power supplies, LED drivers and drives draw current in pulses near the voltage peak. That current contains strong 3rd, 5th and 7th harmonics. In a 3-phase 4-wire system, triplen harmonics (3rd, 9th…) from the three phases do not cancel in the neutral: they add up. A neutral carrying more current than the phases is possible in an office full of IT equipment. That is why such installations often use full-size or oversized neutrals. VFDs are often fitted with DC chokes, line reactors or active front ends to reduce their current THD.

Measuring

Many modern energy meters report voltage THD, current THD and sometimes individual harmonics. A dedicated power quality analyser (IEC 61000-4-30 Class A instruments) records events with timestamps. For a BMS, useful points are: per-phase voltage and its min/max, frequency, voltage and current THD, neutral current and an event counter for sags. Correlating "the chiller tripped at 14:02" with "a 70 % sag at 14:02" ends many arguments.

A lab building suffers unexplained PC reboots several times a month. A Class A analyser left on the sub-board for two weeks records 9 sags to 60–75 % lasting 80–200 ms, matching the times of the neighbouring factory's compressor starts. A line-interactive UPS per lab bench would bridge them; the owner instead chose one online UPS for the lab sub-board.
A cheap offline UPS with stepped output can make some active-PFC power supplies and motors misbehave on battery. For anything other than desktop PCs, choose pure sine output.

Why can the neutral in an IT-heavy office carry more current than any phase?

  • Power quality covers sags, swells, interruptions, harmonics, transients and flicker.
  • EN 50160 sets the grid's expected characteristics, e.g. ±10 % and THD ≤ 8 %.
  • Electronics create harmonics; triplens load the neutral.
  • Log PQ data so trips can be correlated with events.
