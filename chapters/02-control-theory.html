Chapter 02

Control theory for buildings

How controllers decide what to do. From a bimetal thermostat to cascaded PID loops, sequences of operation and hard-wired safety chains. Buildings are slow, noisy and full of disturbances, and that shapes every control choice.

2.1 Open and closed loops

Every control function has the same parts. A setpoint (SP) says what we want. A process variable (PV) is what we measure. The controller computes an output (OP, or manipulated variable) that drives an actuator. Between the actuator and the measurement sits the process: a room, a coil, a pipe network. Disturbances act on the process from outside: sun, people, open windows, wind, a neighbour zone.

Open loop: act without looking

An open-loop controller computes its output from a model or an outside signal, never from the result. A classic building example is weather-compensated flow temperature: the boiler's flow setpoint follows the outdoor temperature along a heating curve. Nothing measures the rooms. A time switch that runs a pump from 06:00 to 18:00 is also open loop.

Open loop is cheap, stable by definition (there is no loop to oscillate) and fast. Its weakness is disturbances: if the sun heats the south façade, the curve does not know, and the rooms overheat.

Closed loop: measure, compare, correct

A closed-loop (feedback) controller subtracts the PV from the SP to get the error e = SP − PV and acts on it. It corrects for any disturbance it can measure the effect of, including ones nobody modelled. The price: a loop can be badly tuned, oscillate or wind up, and it depends on the sensor. A wrong sensor gives a perfectly controlled wrong temperature.

Try it: the same room, two strategies

Feedforward: the best of both

Real systems combine both. Feedforward acts on a measured disturbance before it shows up in the PV (the outdoor temperature sets a base flow temperature), and feedback trims the rest (room sensors correct it). Feedforward does most of the work fast; feedback removes the error the model got wrong.

Open loopClosed loop
Needs a PV sensorNoYes
Corrects unknown disturbancesNoYes
Can oscillateNoYes, if badly tuned
Typical useHeating curves, time programs, fixed fan speedRoom temperature, supply air, duct pressure, CO₂
A typical office building heats with a weather-compensated flow (open loop) in each heating circuit, while thermostatic or electronic room valves close the loop per room. On a sunny March day the south rooms' valves throttle down while the north rooms stay open: the curve gives the base, the room loops trim.

A boiler flow setpoint follows outdoor temperature only. The building fills with 200 people. What happens?

  • SP, PV, OP, error, process, disturbance: the vocabulary of every loop.
  • Open loop is stable and cheap but blind to disturbances.
  • Closed loop corrects disturbances but can oscillate and trusts its sensor.
  • Feedforward plus feedback is the normal pattern in buildings.

2.2 On/off and hysteresis

The simplest closed loop has a binary output: heater on or off, pump on or off, compressor on or off. A naive rule would be "on if PV < SP, else off". With any measurement noise the relay would chatter many times per second. The fix is a hysteresis (switching differential, deadband): switch on below SP − h/2, switch off above SP + h/2, and do nothing in between.

Try it: a thermostat with a deadband

An electric panel heater in a 20 m² room. The sensor has some noise and the heater takes about a minute to warm up, so the temperature always overshoots the switching points slightly. Change the deadband and watch the cycle count.

Choosing the deadband

  • Too narrow: many short cycles. Contacts wear, compressors and burners start too often. A boiler or heat pump forced into many starts per hour loses efficiency and life.
  • Too wide: the room swings and people notice. Comfort complaints start above roughly ±1 K.
  • Typical values: room thermostats 0.3–1 K, DHW cylinders 5–10 K, frost protection 2–3 K.

Equipment adds its own protections on top: minimum on time, minimum off time and maximum starts per hour. Compressor manufacturers commonly allow on the order of 6–10 starts per hour and specify a minimum off time of a few minutes to let pressures equalise. Check the datasheet.

Making on/off look modulating: PWM

Thermal actuators on underfloor manifolds and electric heaters are on/off devices. A controller can still give a 0–100 % demand by pulse-width modulation: within a fixed cycle (often 10–20 minutes for thermal actuators, which take 2–5 minutes to open), the output is on for demand × cycle. Because the process is slow, it only sees the average.

Hysteresis in software and hysteresis in hardware add up. A room controller with 0.5 K hysteresis that drives a boiler with its own 5 K burner differential gives a system nobody designed. Know every deadband in the chain.

A heat pump short-cycles: 14 starts per hour. Which change helps most directly?

  • On/off control needs hysteresis to avoid chattering.
  • Narrow band = comfort and wear; wide band = swings and fewer starts.
  • Process lag causes overshoot past the switching points.
  • PWM turns an on/off actuator into an average-modulating one for slow processes.

2.3 PID: tuning and pitfalls

Most modulating loops in buildings are PI, and some are PID. The controller adds three terms:

OP = Kp · ( e + (1/Ti)·∫e dt + Td · de/dt )

TermReacts toEffectSide effects
P proportional, gain KpError nowBigger error, bigger pushAlone, leaves a steady offset. Too much: oscillation.
I integral, time TiAccumulated errorRemoves the offsetSlows the response, causes overshoot and windup. Shorter Ti = stronger.
D derivative, time TdRate of changeBrakes before overshootAmplifies noise. Rarely used on noisy HVAC signals.

Vendors use different forms. Some give proportional band instead of gain: a band of 5 K means the output goes 0→100 % over 5 K of error, so Kp = 100 % / 5 K = 20 %/K. Some use integral gain Ki = Kp/Ti instead of integral time. Always check the units before copying values between products.

Try it: tune a room heating loop

The simulated process is a room heated through a modulating valve. It is modelled as first order plus dead time: gain 0.12 K per % valve, time constant 10 min, dead time 2 min. At t = 0 the setpoint steps from 17 to 21 °C. At 40 min a window opens and the heat loss rises (a 3 K disturbance).

A practical tuning procedure

  1. Start PI, D = 0. HVAC signals are noisy and processes slow; D rarely pays off.
  2. Do a bump test in manual: step the output 10–20 % and log the PV. Read the process gain K (ΔPV/ΔOP), dead time L (until the PV starts moving) and time constant τ (until 63 % of the change).
  3. Use a model-based rule. The SIMC rule (Skogestad) for a robust PI: pick a closed-loop time τc ≈ L, then Kp = τ / (K·(τc + L)) and Ti = min(τ, 4·(τc + L)). For the simulated room: Kp = 600 / (0.12 · 240) ≈ 21 %/K, Ti = 600 s.
  4. Test with a setpoint step and a disturbance. Aim for little or no overshoot. A comfortable HVAC loop is slower than a "textbook optimal" one.
  5. Retune per season if the gain changes a lot (see below), or use gain scheduling.

Ziegler–Nichols (1942) is still taught. It aims for a quarter-amplitude decay, which is far too aggressive for comfort loops. Use it to get a first idea, then detune.

Pitfalls that cause most real problems

Integral windup

When the output saturates (valve 100 % open, boiler cannot deliver more), the error stays large and the integral keeps growing. When conditions return, the huge stored integral keeps the valve open long after the setpoint is reached: a big overshoot. Fixes: clamp the integrator when the output saturates (conditional integration) or back-calculate it from the limited output. Also freeze or reset the integrator when the plant is off (night, summer), or the loop restarts with a saturated history. Try the "Windup demo" preset with anti-windup on and off.

Non-linear valves and coils

A heating coil delivers most of its capacity in the first 20–30 % of water flow. With a linear valve the loop gain is high at small openings and low near full. An equal-percentage valve characteristic compensates this, which is why it is the default for coil control. Valve authority (pressure drop across the valve versus the whole circuit, usually aimed at 0.5 or more) decides whether the characteristic survives installation.

Oversized actuators and hunting

An oversized valve uses only the first few percent of its stroke. Tiny output changes cause large flow changes and the loop hunts. Hunting is visible on trends as a steady oscillation with a period of a few minutes. It wears actuators quickly.

Sampling, dead time and slow sensors

A sensor polled every 5 minutes over a gateway cannot support a loop with a 2-minute dead time. Run fast loops in the local controller that owns the I/O. The supervisory layer should write setpoints, not close loops over the network.

Bumpless transfer

When an operator switches from manual to automatic, the output must not jump. Good controllers initialise the integrator so that the auto output starts from the manual value.

A typical office AHU supply-air temperature loop on a heating coil runs PI with Kp around 5–15 %/K and Ti of 60–180 s, because the coil responds in tens of seconds. A room temperature loop on a floor heating manifold uses Ti of 30–60 minutes or more. Same algorithm, numbers two orders of magnitude apart.

A P-only room loop settles at 20.4 °C with a 21 °C setpoint, and never closes the gap. Why?

  • P reacts to now, I removes offset, D anticipates. HVAC is mostly PI.
  • Check the form and units: gain or proportional band, Ti or Ki.
  • Bump-test, then use a model-based rule such as SIMC. Aim for no overshoot.
  • Anti-windup, valve characteristic and loop placement cause more trouble than the gains.

2.4 Sequences of operation

Single loops are not enough. Equipment has to be started, proven, run and stopped in the right order, with the right reaction to every failure. A sequence of operation (SOO) is the written description of that behaviour. It is the most important document in a controls project: the engineer writes it, the programmer implements it and the commissioning agent tests against it. ASHRAE Guideline 36 publishes standardised, high-performance sequences for AHUs and terminal units precisely because hand-written sequences vary so much in quality.

Sequences are state machines

Software people will recognise the pattern: a finite set of states, each with defined outputs, and transitions triggered by events, timers and conditions. Write it that way and the gaps show up: what happens if the frost alarm arrives during start-up? Who resets a fault?

What a good sequence spells out

  • Start order and proving: dampers open before fans start (closed dampers plus a running fan can collapse ductwork); airflow proven by a pressure switch within a timeout.
  • Normal control: which loops run, setpoints, resets, the order of heating, heat recovery and cooling stages (never heat and cool at once).
  • Protection: frost, high limits, fire alarm, low pressure. Their priority over normal control.
  • Stop order: fan run-on to dissipate heat from electric heaters, then dampers close.
  • Failure behaviour: what is latched (needs manual reset) and what restarts by itself.
  • Timers and delays: every one with a value. "Wait a while" is not a sequence.

Staging: one loop, several devices

A single PI output often drives several devices in sequence (split range). For a typical AHU tempering loop: 0–33 % heating valve closes, 33–66 % heat-recovery wheel slows and outdoor dampers modulate, 66–100 % cooling valve opens. A deadband between heating and cooling prevents them fighting.

A typical office AHU on a winter morning: the schedule enables it at 06:30, outdoor and exhaust dampers open, the controller waits 30 s for the actuators, starts the fans at minimum speed and waits up to 60 s for the airflow switches. Only then do the temperature loops release. At 03:00 that night the coil water dropped to 4 °C during a pump fault: the unit went to frost protection, closed the outdoor damper, stopped the fans and drove the heating valve fully open.

During "Starting", the frost thermostat trips. The sequence above does not say what to do. What does that reveal?

  • A sequence of operation is a state machine. Write it as one.
  • Prove each step (airflow, flow, position) before the next.
  • Protection has priority in every state. Decide what is latched.
  • Split range sequences heat, recovery and cooling on one loop, with a deadband.

2.5 Cascade and reset control

Many building processes are a chain: the boiler heats water, the water heats a coil or radiator, the air or the room warms up. Controlling the end of the chain with one loop is slow, because every disturbance in the middle has to travel to the room before the controller sees it. Cascade control splits the chain into nested loops.

Rules for cascades

  • The inner loop must be much faster than the outer one: at least 3–5 times, ideally 10.
  • Tune inside out: inner loop first with the outer in manual, then the outer.
  • Limit the inner setpoint: a room loop may ask for 14–28 °C supply air, never 5 °C or 45 °C. These limits protect comfort and stop draughts.
  • Pass saturation upward: if the inner loop is at its limit, the outer integrator must stop too, or it winds up.

Reset control: setpoints that move

A reset (or compensation) moves a setpoint from another variable. It can be open loop (outdoor temperature), closed loop (room demand) or both.

ResetInputMovesWhy
Weather compensation (heating curve)Outdoor temperatureFlow temperatureLower flow = lower losses, condensing boilers and heat pumps run efficiently
Supply air resetZone demand or return airSupply air temperatureAvoid overcooling and reheating
Duct static pressure resetVAV damper positionsFan pressure setpointFan power falls roughly with the cube of speed
Chilled water resetValve positions or outdoorChilled water temperatureEach 1 K higher saves a few % chiller energy

ASHRAE Guideline 36 uses trim and respond for many resets: every few minutes, trim the setpoint a small step towards the efficient side; for each zone that sends a "request" (damper nearly open, too warm), respond with a step back. The setpoint settles where the most demanding zone is just satisfied.

Try it: a heating curve

Underfloor heating typically needs slopes around 0.3–0.6 (flow rarely above 35–40 °C); radiators 1.2–1.8; old buildings with small radiators more. A slope that is too steep overheats in mild weather and too flat underheats in cold. Adjust the slope for cold-day errors and the shift for mild-day errors.

A 40-room hotel with a heat pump and radiators: the heating curve gives 48 °C flow at −10 °C outdoors and 32 °C at +10 °C. Each degree of lower flow temperature raises the heat pump's coefficient of performance by roughly 2–3 %, so a well-set curve with room-feedback trim saves noticeably against a fixed 55 °C flow.

In a cascade, the outer room loop oscillates slowly while the inner supply loop is fine. What is the first thing to check?

  • Cascade: a slow outer loop sets the setpoint of a fast inner loop.
  • Inner much faster than outer. Tune inside out. Limit the inner setpoint.
  • Resets move setpoints for efficiency: heating curves, supply air, duct pressure.
  • Trim and respond lets the most demanding zone set the plant setpoint.

2.6 Thermal inertia and slow systems

A building is a large thermal mass. Concrete, water and air all store heat. When an actuator changes, the temperature responds after a dead time (nothing happens yet) and then approaches its new value exponentially with a time constant τ. After one τ the response has reached 63 %, after three τ 95 %, after five τ about 99 %.

T(t) = T0 + ΔT · (1 − e−(t − L)/τ) for t > L

Emitter / systemTypical dead timeTypical τ (room response)
Fan coil, direct air< 1 min5–15 min
Panel radiator2–5 min20–60 min
Underfloor heating in screed15–60 min2–6 h
Thermally activated concrete slab (TABS)1–3 h8–24 h or more

These ranges depend strongly on construction and are only for orientation.

Try it: step response

Full heat is applied at t = 0 to a room at 17 °C that would end up at 23 °C. Pick an emitter or set your own τ.

What slow systems change

  • Control is slow on purpose. A floor heating loop with Ti of a few minutes will wind up and overshoot for hours. Use long integral times or simple on/off with PWM and a wide cycle.
  • Setbacks pay less. A slab that needs 6 hours to recover saves little from a 4-hour night setback. Use small setbacks (1–2 K) or none, and avoid frequent setpoint changes.
  • Anticipate. Weather forecasts, solar gains and occupancy must be acted on hours ahead. This is where feedforward and model predictive control (MPC) earn their keep.
  • Overshoot is expensive. Once a heavy floor is too warm, nothing removes the heat quickly, and opening windows wastes it.
  • Self-regulation helps. A floor at 24 °C in a room at 22 °C emits little; if the room warms by sun, the output drops automatically. Low-temperature systems are partly self-regulating.
Do not judge a slow loop after 15 minutes. When commissioning floor heating, log for at least a day before changing parameters, and change one thing at a time. Most "the floor heating doesn't work" complaints in the first week come from impatience or from too-short integral times.
A 40-room hotel with underfloor heating in the bathrooms and radiators in the bedrooms. The radiators can follow occupancy: setback when the room is unsold, back to comfort within about an hour of check-in. The bathroom floors run at a near-constant setpoint all day, because they could not recover in time anyway.

A system has τ = 3 h and no dead time. Roughly how long until it reaches 95 % of a step?

  • Dead time plus time constant describe most building responses.
  • 63 % after τ, 95 % after 3τ, 99 % after 5τ.
  • Heavy systems need slow tuning, small setbacks and anticipation.

2.7 Schedules, occupancy, modes

The cheapest kilowatt-hour is the one not used in an empty building. Before any loop runs, something decides which setpoint applies now. That is the job of schedules, occupancy detection and operating modes.

Operating modes

Most room and plant controllers offer a small set of modes. Names differ by vendor but the idea is common (EN ISO 16484 and the KNX/BACnet HVAC profiles use similar sets):

ModeHeating SPCooling SPWhen
Comfort21 °C24 °COccupied
Standby / pre-comfort19 °C26 °CScheduled occupied, nobody present yet
Economy / night16–17 °C28 °CUnoccupied
Building protection5–8 °C35 °CLong absence, window open

Values are typical examples. The gap between heating and cooling setpoints is a zero-energy band: inside it, neither runs.

Where the mode comes from: priorities

Several sources compete to set a mode. Controllers resolve them by priority, much like BACnet's 16-level priority array for commands (priority 1 is highest, used for life safety; 8 for manual operator; 16 lowest default).

  1. Protection (frost, window contact, fire) overrides everything.
  2. Manual override by an operator, usually time-limited.
  3. Local user override (a "comfort for 2 h" button).
  4. Occupancy sensor: present or absent.
  5. Time schedule and calendar (holidays, events).
Manual overrides without an expiry time are one of the biggest sources of wasted energy in real buildings. Someone fixes a complaint at 3 a.m. by forcing Comfort, and it stays that way for a year. Always give overrides a timeout and report active overrides.

Try it: a weekly schedule with optimal start

Click or drag across cells to toggle occupied hours. The amber hours before each occupied block are the optimal start pre-heat, which gets longer as the outdoor temperature drops.

Optimal start and optimal stop

A fixed early start time is set for the coldest day and wastes energy on every other day. Optimal start computes when to start so that the room reaches comfort exactly at occupancy. Simple versions use a learned heat-up rate in K/h that depends on the outdoor temperature; better ones adapt from each morning's result. Optimal stop does the reverse: switch the plant off early and coast on the stored heat until the end of occupancy, while staying within a tolerance (for example 0.5 K below comfort).

Occupancy-based control

  • PIR sensors detect motion. Cheap but miss people sitting still; use a hold time of 15–30 min.
  • Presence / mmWave radar detects small movements and breathing. Better for offices and hotel rooms.
  • CO₂ tracks the number of people over time. Good for demand-controlled ventilation, not for fast lighting.
  • Key cards, room booking, access control, Wi-Fi counts: business data that says who is expected.

Combine sources: the schedule says when people may be there, occupancy sensors say whether they are. Standby when scheduled but absent; Comfort only when both agree.

A 40-room hotel links its property management system to the room controllers. Unsold rooms sit in Economy. On check-in the room goes to Standby; when the key card is inserted, Comfort. When the guest leaves the room, Standby again after 20 minutes. On check-out, back to Economy.

An operator forced a meeting room to Comfort during an event. Six months later it is still in Comfort. What design rule was missing?

  • Modes (Comfort, Standby, Economy, Protection) decide which setpoints apply.
  • Resolve competing sources by priority. Protection wins. Overrides expire.
  • Optimal start and stop adapt to the weather instead of fixed times.
  • Schedule says may be occupied; sensors say is occupied.

2.8 Interlocks and safety chains

An interlock is a condition that must be true before something may run, or that stops it. Some are about plant sense (do not run the boiler pump when the boiler is off). Others are about safety: a fan must stop on a fire alarm, a burner must stop on overheating. The two kinds are built differently.

Software interlockHard-wired safety chain
WhereIn controller logicContacts in series in the control circuit
Works if controller crashesNoYes
Typical usePump before boiler, dampers before fans, no heat with coolingEmergency stop, overheat cut-out, fire alarm stop, motor protection
CertifiedRarelyOften required (e.g. safety temperature limiters to EN 14597)

Fail-safe: the chain must break to stop

A safety chain is a string of normally closed contacts in series feeding the coil of a contactor. Every contact must be closed for the motor to run. A broken wire, a lost supply or an open contact all stop the plant. That is the fail-safe principle (closed-circuit or "Ruhestrom" principle): the safe state needs no energy and no working component. A chain built from normally open contacts that must close to stop would silently fail to stop when a wire breaks.

Try it: an AHU supply fan safety chain

Click a contact to open it. The chain is 24 V AC from the control transformer through each contact to the fan contactor coil. Each contact also has a monitoring input on the controller, so the BMS knows which one opened.

Latching and reset

Some devices reset themselves when the condition clears; others latch and need a person. A safety temperature limiter (STB, high-limit) on a boiler latches by design: a boiler that overheated should be looked at before it fires again. Frost thermostats on AHUs are often configured to latch for the same reason. A rule of thumb: if the trip means something is broken, latch; if it means a condition is temporary, auto-reset.

The BMS may show the state and the reset request, but resetting a safety device remotely is generally forbidden or tightly restricted: someone must look at the plant first.

A typical office AHU: the fire alarm panel opens a volt-free contact in the fan chain, which stops both fans within a second, independently of the AHU controller. The controller sees the monitoring input, raises a "fire stop" alarm, closes the dampers and stays in a fault state until the fire panel resets. The BMS only reports it.

A designer wires the fire-alarm stop as a normally open contact that closes to drop the fan. What is wrong?

  • Software interlocks enforce plant logic; hard-wired chains enforce safety.
  • Safety chains use normally closed contacts in series: break to stop.
  • Latch trips that mean something is broken. Reset locally, not from the BMS.
  • Monitor each contact so the BMS can say which one opened.
